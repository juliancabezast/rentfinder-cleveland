Landlord and Investor Resources for Cleveland Rental Owners

This is the index to everything we publish for owners of rental property in Greater Cleveland. Four bodies of rules govern your house at once: the City of Cleveland's registration and lead-safe ordinances, Cuyahoga County's tax and disclosure requirements, Ohio's landlord-tenant and rental-disclosure statutes, and — if you take a voucher — the housing authority's own process. Every figure below is quoted from the office that publishes it, and linked at the bottom.

Start here: the four decisions every Cleveland owner faces

Almost every question an owner sends us reduces to one of four decisions, and each one has a Cleveland-specific answer that is different from the generic advice you will find nationally.

1. Will you take a housing voucher? In the City of Cleveland this is genuinely your call. The city's Fair Housing Office lists thirteen protected classes — "Age, Ancestry, Color, Disability, Ethnic group, Familial status, Gender identity, Marital Status, National origin, Race, Religion, Sex, Sexual orientation" — and source of income is not among them, so declining a voucher in Cleveland is lawful. Lorain went the other way: the city "added 'source of income' as a protected class in its fair housing ordinance Chapter 136 ... in 2024," which "prohibits landlords" from denying housing to a household paying with a Housing Choice Voucher. Wisconsin has no statewide source-of-income protection either. Our own posture is settled: we accept vouchers on every house we manage, and we think the arithmetic favors it — see Is Section 8 a good investment in Cleveland.

2. Will you self-manage? The honest test is not whether you can — it is whether you will be in the county when the furnace dies in January. If you are not, Ohio decides part of this for you (see decision 4). Start with Is a property manager worth it in Cleveland and how much time managing a rental actually takes.

3. What will you spend on turnover? The number that decides this is not the rehab quote — it is the daily cost of the vacancy you are financing while the work happens. We break it out in what a vacant rental day costs and the hidden costs of renting out a house here.

4. Who is your local agent? Ohio Revised Code 5323.03 is blunt about out-of-state owners: an owner who resides outside the state "shall designate ... an individual who resides in the state to serve as the owner's agent for the acceptance of service of process." Cleveland stacks its own version on top, requiring a Local Agent in Charge from owners "NOT located in Cuyahoga, Summit, Lake, Portage, Medina, Lorain, or Geauga County." Full walkthrough: owning a Cleveland rental from out of state.

Section 8 and CMHA: RFTA, inspections, payment standards and HAP

Cuyahoga Metropolitan Housing Authority (CMHA) administers Housing Choice Vouchers for Cuyahoga County; in Milwaukee the equivalent authority is HACM. A voucher tenancy is not a different kind of lease — it is your ordinary lease plus a federal tenancy addendum, an inspection, a rent-reasonableness test and a contract between you and the authority.

City rules: rental registration, lead-safe certification and suburban ordinances

This is the layer that generates violations, because it is invisible until it is not.

Cleveland rental registration. "All owners of non-owner-occupied residential properties are required to register. The fee is $70 per unit and includes all properties that the owner is not living in." The city adds that this "may include units occupied by family members and friends even if nothing of value is received in exchange" — so the house you let your brother live in is a registered rental. Renewals open January 1, "All fees must be paid by March 31st," and for a new unit: "Rentals must be registered before tenants occupy the property." Detail: what a Cleveland rental registration permit is.

Lead Safe certification. Cleveland City Council "in 2019 passed legislation that requires owners of rental property in the City to prove that their dwelling units are safe from lead hazards." It applies to rental units built before 1978. There is "no fee for application," certification "must be renewed every two years," and risk-assessment "[r]eports must be submitted no later than 90 days after the inspection date." Practical version: lead paint rules for Cleveland rentals.

Two more Cleveland triggers. Buildings with four or more units need an HVAC certificate; where each unit has its own system, "10% of the HVAC systems need to be inspected, with a minimum of 5 units inspected." And to receive a Certificate Approving Rental Occupancy, "your property cannot have open code violations" and you must "provide proof that your property taxes are paid in full or that you are on a payment plan with the County in good standing."

The county filing everyone forgets. Separately from any city registration, ORC 5323.02 requires an owner of residential rental property to file name, address and telephone number with the county auditor, and "shall update the information ... within sixty days after any change." Cuyahoga is explicit that the two are not substitutes: "You must disclose your residential rental property to Cuyahoga County even if you have registered your rental property with the municipality where it is located." It is one-time and free; skipping it can cost "a penalty of $50 following each tax bill," and the state statute allows a special assessment of "not less than fifty dollars or more than one hundred fifty dollars."

Outside Cleveland, the calendar changes. Akron's registration deadline is January 31, at "$25.00 per unit ($2,500 maximum)" with a "$25.00" late fee. Lakewood licenses housing annually with a November 1 deadline, "$75.00 per unit" for non-owner-occupied one-or-two-family structures, "$200.00 per building" for a vacant property licence, and a "$25.00 per unit" late fee — and Lakewood fees "are not pro-rated." Lorain requires you to file the names and addresses of each new tenant with the Administrator "[w]ithin thirty days after a new tenant occupies" the unit, under ordinance 192.201.

Money: property taxes, city income tax, turnover cost and the cost of a vacant day

Property tax. Ohio taxes 35% of market value, not 100%: the taxable value of a parcel "shall be thirty-five per cent of the 'true value in money'." Cuyahoga bills in arrears — "charges payable in 2026 are for tax year 2025" — in two halves, due February 19, 2026 and August 13, 2026, with a 10% penalty on March 2 and August 23 respectively. Two traps: the Treasurer says "Postmark is not accepted for late payment," and the 2024 sexennial reappraisal moved values hard — "an average increase of 32% ... ranging from 15% in Hunting Valley to 67% in East Cleveland." Full mechanics and the appeal window: Cuyahoga County property taxes for landlords.

Municipal income tax. Rental income is municipal income. CCA's rule is that you must file if you "[o]wn rental property in a CCA member municipality and charge gross monthly rent exceeding $125.00." Cleveland's rate is 2.50%. If you report the rental on a federal Schedule E, you file the Individual CCA form, not the Net Profit form — and note that "[n]et operating losses cannot be used to offset wages or W-2 income." Estimated payments start once the liability reaches $200.

Federal. The building depreciates over 27.5 years; the land does not. The IRS's own example is a $185,000 house where "the building cost $160,000 and the land cost $25,000," giving a first-year deduction of "$5,091." More: rental income taxes and cash flow on a Cleveland rental.

The number people skip. A vacant day is a real expense with no offsetting revenue: taxes, insurance and utilities keep running. We size it in what a vacant rental day costs in Cleveland, and list the line items owners forget in hidden costs.

Leasing: pricing the rent, marketing, screening and days on market

Our typical Cleveland house rents between $700 and $1,800 a month. Pricing inside that band is not a guess — on a voucher tenancy it is bounded by the authority's rent-reasonableness test, and on a market tenancy by what comparable unassisted units actually let for. Legal: evictions, deposits, tenant damage and ending a tenancy

Deposits. ORC 5321.16 sets two obligations Ohio owners routinely miss. First, interest: a deposit "in excess of fifty dollars or one month's periodic rent, whichever is greater, shall bear interest on the excess at the rate of five per cent per annum if the tenant remains in possession ... for six months or more." Second, the clock: deductions must be itemized in writing and delivered "within thirty days after termination of the rental agreement and delivery of possession," and if you miss it the tenant "may recover the property and money due him, together with damages in an amount equal to the amount wrongfully withheld, and reasonable attorneys fees." See security deposits and tenant damage in Ohio.

Entry. ORC 5321.04 requires reasonable notice except in an emergency, and "[t]wenty-four hours is presumed to be a reasonable notice in the absence of evidence to the contrary." Turn up unannounced and the statute is not on your side.

Eviction in Cleveland. The city passed Pay to Stay on August 10, 2022: "if a tenant is summoned to court for an eviction, they can stop the proceeding if they have the full amount of rent owed, plus any late fees," and Cleveland's version is broader than the state model — the city describes an "expanded definition of tender in the ordinance to include any emergency rental assistance vouchers from an accredited social service, nonprofit, governmental, or quasi-governmental agency that guarantees the payment of past due rent, reasonable late fees, and court costs." In practice this means a rent case can be cured on the courthouse steps by a third-party payer. Budget accordingly: what an eviction costs a Cleveland landlord, and for voucher tenancies, how to end a tenancy with a voucher tenant.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a rental registration to rent out a house in Cleveland?
Yes. The City of Cleveland requires all owners of non-owner-occupied residential properties to register, at $70 per unit, and states that "Rentals must be registered before tenants occupy the property." The requirement covers units occupied by family or friends even when nothing of value is received in exchange. Separately, ORC 5323.02 requires you to file owner contact information with the county auditor — Cuyahoga confirms that is required "even if you have registered your rental property with the municipality."
How much does property management cost in Cleveland?
Ours is 5% of monthly rent, charged only while the unit is rented; tenant placement is one month's rent and only if we place the tenant; attending an inspection and writing you a report is $100; repair estimates are free and itemized within 24 hours. Other companies price differently — flat fee, percentage, leasing fee, renewal fee — so compare the whole schedule, not the headline percentage.
Should I accept Section 8 on my Cleveland rental?
It is your decision. Source of income is not among the thirteen protected classes listed by the City of Cleveland's fair housing office, so an owner in the city may lawfully decline a voucher. Lorain is different: it added source of income as a protected class in Chapter 136 of its fair housing ordinance in 2024. Our position is pro-voucher, and we accept vouchers on every house we manage.
How long does it take to rent out a house in Cleveland?
We do not publish an average, because we will not publish a number we cannot source. What we can tell you is what controls the clock: the unit has to be registered before a tenant occupies it, a pre-1978 unit needs its Lead Safe certification, and on a voucher tenancy the housing authority's inspection sits between the signed lease and the first payment.
Do you manage properties outside Cleveland?
Yes — Cleveland and East Cleveland, Akron, Lorain, Elyria and Milwaukee, Wisconsin. Each city has its own leasing agent, and the rules differ: Akron's rental registration deadline is January 31, Lakewood's housing licence runs to November 1, and Lorain requires new tenants to be reported within thirty days.
