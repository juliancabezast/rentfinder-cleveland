How Long Does a Landlord Have to Make Repairs in Ohio?

Ohio does not start a repair clock when you complain — it starts when you give written notice. ORC 5321.07(A) requires notice in writing "specifying the acts, omissions, or code violations that constitute noncompliance." From there, 5321.07(B) opens your remedies once the landlord fails to remedy the condition "within a reasonable time … or within thirty days, whichever is sooner." Thirty days is the ceiling; for no heat or no water, a reasonable time is much shorter. And you must be current on rent to use any of it.

What the landlord must maintain under ORC 5321.04

Before the clock, the duty. ORC 5321.04 lists what a landlord in Ohio shall do, and the first several items are the ones that come up in Cleveland rentals week after week:

  • (A)(1) — "Comply with the requirements of all applicable building, housing, health, and safety codes that materially affect health and safety." Read the qualifier at the end: this is the hook that turns a City of Cleveland code violation into a lease problem rather than a merely municipal one, but it reaches the violations that materially affect health and safety, not every line on an inspector's sheet.
  • (A)(2) — "Make all repairs and do whatever is reasonably necessary to put and keep the premises in a fit and habitable condition." The catch-all.
  • (A)(3) — "Keep all common areas of the premises in a safe and sanitary condition." In a Cleveland two-family or four-suite, this is the shared stairwell, the back porch, the basement laundry.
  • (A)(4) — "Maintain in good and safe working order and condition all electrical, plumbing, sanitary, heating, ventilating, and air conditioning fixtures and appliances, and elevators, supplied or required to be supplied by the landlord." Two things to read carefully. The duty is to maintain, not merely to respond after failure. And it covers what the landlord supplies or is required to supply — a window air conditioner you bought and installed yourself is not in it.
  • (A)(6) — "Supply running water, reasonable amounts of hot water, and reasonable heat at all times, except where the building that includes the dwelling unit is not required by law to be equipped for that purpose, or the dwelling unit is so constructed that heat or hot water is generated by an installation within the exclusive control of the tenant and supplied by a direct public utility connection."

That exception at the end of (A)(6) matters a lot in Cleveland's older housing stock, where a converted double often has one gas meter per suite and a furnace inside the unit that the tenant controls and pays for directly. In that arrangement the supply obligation reads differently than it does in a heat-included building. Read your lease and look at whose name is on the meter before you assume.

The 30-day clock and how written notice starts it

Here is the mechanism most renters get wrong. Text messages to a maintenance line, phone calls, and complaints made in person may all be perfectly good ways to get something fixed — but under ORC 5321.07(A) the thing that arms your legal remedies is written notice: "The tenant may give notice in writing to the landlord, specifying the acts, omissions, or code violations that constitute noncompliance."

Then 5321.07(B): the remedies become available if the landlord, "[a]fter receipt of the notice fails to remedy the condition within a reasonable time … or within thirty days, whichever is sooner."

Read "whichever is sooner" twice. The thirty days is a cap on how long a landlord can take, not a grant of thirty days for every problem. A loose cabinet door may well be a thirty-day item. A dead furnace in January, sewage backing into a basement, or no running water is not — for those, a reasonable time is measured in hours or a day or two, and the reasonable-time branch governs because it is the sooner of the two.

When the clock is shorter: heat, running water and other essential services

Nothing in Chapter 5321 publishes a shorter numeric deadline for essential services. What it gives you instead is the reasonable-time standard, plus the separate (A)(6) supply obligation and the (A)(4) duty to keep heating fixtures in good and safe working order. Practically, that means the argument you are making is not "the statute says 24 hours" — it is "thirty days is plainly unreasonable for this condition, and the statute says whichever is sooner."

Two Cleveland-specific facts strengthen that argument. First, the City treats loss of heat as a code matter in its own right: Cleveland's 311 service catalog carries a dedicated "No Heat" request whose stated purpose is to "[r]eport a broken heating system or gas is shut off," and which "makes sure buildings meet code." Second, the City requires owners of rental buildings with four or more units to file a heating-system certification with the Department of Building and Housing, on a form that cites CCO 365.02(d)(1) and carries a checkbox for "NOT OPERATING / NO HEAT." A landlord who has certified that the system works has a hard time treating its failure as a routine ticket.

How to write the notice so it counts (and how to deliver it)

The statute asks for one thing: specificity. "Specifying the acts, omissions, or code violations that constitute noncompliance" is the test, and a notice that says "the apartment is in bad shape" does not meet it. Write it like an inspector would.

  • Date it. The date is the whole point — it is what the thirty days runs from.
  • Name the condition and the location. "The furnace in the basement has not produced heat since February 3. Thermostat set to 70; no ignition." Not "it's cold."
  • Tie it to a duty where you can. Citing ORC 5321.04(A)(4) or (A)(6) costs you one line and signals that you know the notice has legal effect.
  • List every open item in one notice. One dated document covering five conditions is far stronger than five texts spread over two months.
  • Ask for a specific remedy and keep the tone flat. You are creating an exhibit, not winning an argument.
  • Send it where the statute says, and send it provably. ORC 5321.07(A) is explicit about the destination: "The notice shall be sent to the person or place where rent is normally paid." Not the maintenance line, not a leasing agent's cell phone — the person or place that takes your rent. On method the statute is silent, so choose one that leaves a record: certified mail with return receipt is cleanest, email self-timestamps, hand delivery works if you photograph the signed copy. Keep a copy of what you sent, not just proof that you sent something.

Then keep paying rent, on time, in the normal way, while the clock runs. That single habit protects everything that follows.

When the clock runs out: what escrow is, in one paragraph

The first of the three statutory remedies is the one people mean when they say "escrow." ORC 5321.07(B)(1) lets the tenant "[d]eposit all rent that is due and thereafter becomes due the landlord with the clerk of the municipal or county court." Note the words all and thereafter becomes due: this is not partial payment and it is not a one-time gesture. In Cleveland that clerk is the Cleveland Municipal Court, and its Housing Division — the Cleveland Housing Court — is where these matters are heard. Before you deposit anything, call the Housing Court at (216) 664-4295 and ask the Housing Court Specialists; depositing in the wrong place or the wrong amount is the one mistake that turns a repair case into a nonpayment case.

The full walkthrough lives on its own page. Filing mechanics, what the court can order, which conditions qualify and the ways tenants get it wrong are all in rent escrow in Ohio — depositing rent with the court. This page is about the clock that has to run out first.

The other two remedies: court order to repair, and terminating the lease

Escrow is not the only option, and for some conditions it is not the best one.

  • ORC 5321.07(B)(2) — "Apply to the court for an order directing the landlord to remedy the condition." This is the remedy that produces a repaired furnace rather than a pot of money, and it carries more than the headline sentence suggests. The division goes on: "As part of the application, the tenant may deposit rent pursuant to division (B)(1) of this section, may apply for an order reducing the periodic rent due the landlord until the landlord remedies the condition, and may apply for an order to use the rent deposited to remedy the condition." A rent reduction while the condition persists, and repairs paid out of the deposited rent, are both things you have to ask the court for — they do not happen automatically, and no one will offer them to you.
  • ORC 5321.07(B)(3) — terminate the rental agreement. The exit. Worth pairing with our guide on breaking a lease in Ohio so you understand what termination does to your deposit and your last month.

Whatever you choose, ORC 5321.12 sits behind all of it: "[i]n any action under Chapter 5321. of the Revised Code, any party may recover damages for the breach of contract or the breach of any duty that is imposed by law."

Three things that switch 5321.07 off before you start

The remedies come with conditions, and they disqualify more tenants than any other part of the chapter:

  • You must be current in rent payments. That requirement lives in 5321.07(B) itself, which opens the remedies only "if the tenant is current in rent payments due under the rental agreement." If you stopped paying to force the repair, you have removed your own footing. Pay first, then use the statute.
  • Some very small landlords sit outside the section. ORC 5321.07(C): "This section does not apply to any landlord who is a party to rental agreements that cover three or fewer dwelling units and who provides notice of that fact in a written rental agreement or, in the case of an oral tenancy, delivers written notice of that fact to the tenant at the time of initial occupancy by the tenant." Read the test as written — it is not how many units the landlord owns, it is how many dwelling units the landlord's rental agreements cover, and the notice of that fact has to have actually been given. Check your lease for that clause. In a city with as many owner-occupied doubles and small portfolios as Cleveland, this exception is not theoretical.
  • Student tenants are carved out — but the term is narrow. ORC 5321.07(D): "This section does not apply to a dwelling unit occupied by a student tenant." Before you assume that is you, read the definition in ORC 5321.01, which limits a "student tenant" to "[a] person who occupies a dwelling unit owned or operated by the college or university at which the person is a student, and who has a rental agreement that is contingent upon the person's status as a student." A CWRU or Cleveland State student renting a private apartment off campus is not a student tenant under that definition and keeps the full section; someone in university-owned housing on a status-contingent agreement does not.

If any of the three applies, you still have the underlying 5321.04 duties, ORC 5321.12 damages, and the code-enforcement route below. What you lose is the 5321.07 machinery.

The duty that runs the other way, and can stop your clock

A repair claim gets weaker fast if the condition is one ORC 5321.05 puts on you — keeping your own part of the premises "safe and sanitary," disposing of rubbish and garbage, and, where the written rental agreement so requires, maintaining the range, refrigerator, washer, dryer or dishwasher the landlord supplied. And 5321.05(B) requires you to let the landlord in to do the work. Refusing access while demanding the repair is the fastest way to lose; the thirty days is not running while the technician cannot get through the door.

The full tenant-duty list, side by side with the landlord's, is in Ohio tenant rights when repairs are ignored. Where to report a code violation in Cleveland while you wait

The 5321.07 track and the city track run in parallel, and running both is usually right — a code inspector's written finding is exactly the kind of "code violation" the statute asks you to specify.

In Cleveland, building and housing complaints go through 311 — City of Cleveland Citizen Support Specialists at 3-1-1 or (216) 664-2000 — including the dedicated No Heat request described above. The City's stated process is that once a complaint is received, an inspector conducts an investigation to determine whether there is an ordinance violation. If you smell gas, call 911, not 311.

Code enforcement has real weight here. The Cleveland Housing Court states that a housing code violation charged as a first-degree misdemeanor can carry a "$1,000 fine and 180 days in jail," with a minor-degree violation carrying a $100 fine. That is the leverage a code case adds to a repair case.

One last protection to know before you dial. Ohio Revised Code 5321.02 bars a landlord from retaliating against you — by raising your rent, by cutting back services owed to you, or by bringing or threatening an action for possession — because you complained to an appropriate governmental agency about a building, housing, health or safety code violation at the premises (official text). Read the limit the statute attaches, because it is easy to miss: the complaint has to be about a violation that materially affects health and safety. Complaining to the city is not, by itself, a blanket shield — the condition you reported has to clear that bar. Where it does and retaliation happens anyway, division (B) of the same section makes the retaliation a defense to an eviction and supports actual damages plus reasonable attorney's fees.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a landlord have to make repairs in Ohio?
ORC 5321.07(B) opens the tenant's remedies once the landlord, after receiving written notice, fails to remedy the condition "within a reasonable time" or "within thirty days, whichever is sooner." Thirty days is the outer limit, not the target — for a condition like no heat or no running water, a reasonable time is far shorter, and it is the shorter of the two that governs.
Can I stop paying rent if my landlord will not fix something?
No. Ohio's remedy is to deposit the rent with the clerk of court, not to keep it. ORC 5321.07(B) opens the remedies only if the tenant is current in rent payments due under the rental agreement, so withholding rent outright forfeits the very protection you are trying to invoke and exposes you to an eviction for nonpayment.
How does rent escrow work in Cleveland?
Under ORC 5321.07(B)(1) the tenant deposits all rent that is due and thereafter becomes due with the clerk of the municipal or county court. The Cleveland Housing Court confirms on its FAQ that a tenant may deposit rent with the court and directs tenants to its Housing Court Specialists for the procedure. The court does not publish the fees or filing deadlines for that process online.
Can I repair it myself and deduct the cost from rent in Ohio?
Not on your own initiative. ORC 5321.07(B) lists three remedies — deposit rent with the clerk of court, apply to the court for an order directing the landlord to remedy the condition, or terminate the rental agreement — and self-help repair-and-deduct is not among them, so hiring a plumber and short-paying rent leaves you with no statutory shield. The court route gets you close, though: under (B)(2) a tenant applying for a repair order "may apply for an order reducing the periodic rent due the landlord until the landlord remedies the condition, and may apply for an order to use the rent deposited to remedy the condition." Repairs paid out of deposited rent exist — by court order, not by your own arithmetic.
What if the repair is an emergency like no heat?
The statutory standard is what does the work: ORC 5321.07(B) gives the landlord a reasonable time or thirty days, whichever is sooner, and for an emergency it is the reasonable-time branch that governs. No heat in a Cleveland January is not a thirty-day condition. Report it to the landlord in writing immediately, and report it to the City of Cleveland separately — a no-heat service request goes to Building and Housing at (216) 664-2000, and gas odor is a 911 call.
