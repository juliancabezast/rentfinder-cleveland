How to Prove Income to Rent in Ohio Without Pay Stubs

No pay stubs is not a dead end in Ohio. Landlords accept substitutes that answer the same three questions a stub answers — who pays you, how much, and will it continue: three months of bank statements, last year's tax return with Schedule C, an official benefit award letter, a signed offer letter, or an employer letter on letterhead with a phone number that gets answered. No Ohio statute dictates which documents a landlord must take, so the winning move is to hand over a packet that is easy to verify rather than arguing about the one document you do not have.

Updated August 8, 2026 ·8 min read ·By the Rent Finder Cleveland team

What a landlord is actually testing when they ask for income

Not your job title, and not really the number either. Three things:

  1. Is it real? Can someone independent confirm it — an agency, a bank, an employer who answers the phone.
  2. Is it current? A 2024 tax return alone says what happened two years ago.
  3. Will it continue? Rent is due twelve more times.

A pay stub happens to answer all three in one page, which is the only reason it is the default. Once you understand that, substituting becomes mechanical: pick documents that cover all three between them. A tax return covers real, bank statements cover current, an employer letter covers will continue. Together they beat a single stub.

Worth knowing what the landlord may pull alongside your documents: under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, a landlord may obtain a consumer report on you because they have a legitimate business need for the information ... in connection with a business transaction that is initiated by the consumer (15 U.S.C. 1681b). Before you apply anywhere, pull your own file. The FCRA requires nationwide agencies to disclose your file once during any 12-month period upon request of the consumer and without charge (15 U.S.C. 1681j). Fixing a stale collection before an application beats explaining it after a denial.

Bank statements: how many months, and what the landlord reads in them

Three months is the standard ask in Greater Cleveland, and there is no Ohio law behind it — it is just long enough to distinguish a pattern from a good week.

Send complete PDF statements downloaded from your bank, all pages, including the ones with nothing on them. App screenshots get rejected constantly, and fairly: a screenshot proves nothing about who issued it.

What a reviewer actually looks at:

  • Recurring deposits of similar size on a similar cadence. Consistency beats size. A steady $2,100 a month reads better than $900, $4,400, $600.
  • Who the deposits come from. A named payer — an employer, an agency, a platform — is verification. A pile of cash deposits is not, because a bank cannot tell where cash came from.
  • Whether the balance survives the month. If the account is at zero on the 28th every month, income size is not really the issue.
  • Overdraft frequency. One is noise, six a month is a pattern.

If you deposit cash, do it consistently and label it — deposit the same day each pay period so the statement shows a rhythm a reviewer can read. Random cash deposits are the least useful income evidence there is.

Self-employed, 1099 and gig work: tax returns, Schedule C and app earnings reports

Cleveland has a lot of self-employed renters — trades, home health, delivery, rideshare, hair, hauling. The packet that works:

  • Last filed tax return with the Schedule C. Send the return, not a summary. Note that Schedule C shows net profit after deductions, which is often far below what you actually collect. Say that out loud in your cover note and show the gross receipts line.
  • Three months of business bank statements — ideally a separate business account. If your business and personal money live in one account, expect more questions, and pre-empt them by highlighting the business deposits.
  • Platform earnings reports. Rideshare, delivery and marketplace apps all export a downloadable earnings statement with your name on it. Export the PDF; do not screenshot the dashboard.
  • 1099-NEC forms from your major clients for the prior year.
  • Signed client contracts if you have recurring work — this is your will it continue document, and self-employed applicants almost always forget it.

One Cleveland-specific note: if your work is seasonal — roofing, concrete, landscaping, snow — a three-month window in February tells a false story. Send twelve months of statements plus the tax return, and state the seasonality in one sentence at the top. Reviewers are not hostile to seasonal income; they are hostile to surprises.

Benefit income that counts: Social Security, SSI, SSDI, VA, pension, unemployment, child support

Benefit income is often the easiest income to verify in the entire application, because the payer is a government agency that issues a document stating the amount. The kinds landlords in this market routinely see documented:

  • Social Security retirement, SSI and SSDI
  • VA benefits and military retirement
  • Private or public pensions
  • Unemployment compensation
  • Child support and spousal support, where there is a court order or a state disbursement record
  • A Housing Choice Voucher, which is not income to you but changes the rent you owe — see below

On whether a landlord must count these: we found no Ohio statute that lists which income types a private landlord is required to accept. What is clearly prohibited is differential treatment. Federal regulation makes it unlawful to use different qualification criteria or applications, or sale or rental standards or procedures, such as income standards, application requirements, application fees, credit analysis because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin (24 CFR 100.60), and disability is likewise protected under Ohio law (ORC 4112.02). A landlord who counts wage income but discounts disability income is standing on thin ice. For our part: we count verifiable benefit income, full stop.

How to pull an official award letter from SSA or Ohio Job and Family Services

An award letter — Social Security calls it a benefit verification letter — is the single strongest document a benefit recipient can hand a landlord, because it comes from the payer and states the amount in writing.

Request it from the agency that pays you, through that agency's own .gov site or its published phone line. For Social Security, SSI and SSDI, that is the Social Security Administration; for unemployment and Ohio public assistance benefits, that is the Ohio Department of Job and Family Services. We do not reprint agency phone numbers or step-by-step screens here, because those change and a stale phone number in a housing article is worse than no phone number — go to the agency site directly and use the contact route it publishes today.

Whatever route you use, the letter a landlord can actually use has five things on it:

  1. The issuing agency name and letterhead
  2. Your full legal name
  3. The benefit type
  4. The monthly gross amount
  5. The period it covers, or a statement that it continues

Two practical warnings. First, a bank app screenshot showing a deposit is not an award letter — it proves money arrived, not that it will arrive again. Second, request it before you start touring. These letters take days to arrive by mail, and a house you love will not wait for the postal service.

Paid in cash: employer letters, notarized statements and what makes them credible

Getting paid in cash is common in Cleveland trades and service work, and it is the hardest case — not because anyone assumes bad faith, but because cash leaves no third-party trail.

What makes an employer letter credible:

  • Company letterhead, or at minimum the business name, address and EIN.
  • Specifics: your job title, hourly rate or salary, average weekly hours, start date, and whether the position is ongoing.
  • A named signer with a title and a direct phone number that a stranger calling at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday will actually reach.
  • Corroboration: bank deposits matching the stated pay, or last year's tax return reporting that income.

Notarization proves the person signed it — it does not prove the contents are true — so it helps at the margins and never substitutes for a working phone number. And do not overstate. Screening companies verify, and an income figure that does not survive a phone call is far more damaging than a modest number that does.

Housing Choice Voucher holders: how the voucher changes the income math

If you hold a voucher, the income question is different, because you are not being asked to cover the whole rent.

  • CMHA states that a family is generally required to pay approximately 30% of adjusted monthly income toward rent and utilities (cmha.net).
  • At initial occupancy, where the gross rent exceeds the payment standard, federal rule caps the family share of rent and utilities at 40 percent of the family's adjusted monthly income (24 CFR 982.508).
  • The payment standard is not the maximum amount that the landlord can charge; it is the maximum amount of subsidy CMHA pays (cmha.net). Since January 1, 2025 CMHA applies Small Area Fair Market Rent payment standards, which vary by ZIP code.
  • Federal rule also protects you from the housing authority's own paperwork problems: if the PHA fails to pay the housing assistance payment to the owner, that failure is not a violation of your lease, and the owner may not terminate your tenancy over it (24 CFR 982.310).

Practically: a landlord applying an income multiple usually applies it to your portion, which is the portion you actually pay. Bring the voucher, and bring income documentation anyway — the housing authority verified your income for the subsidy, not for the lease. On the legal status of source-of-income protection in Cleveland, read source of income discrimination in Cleveland, Ohio; the short version is in the FAQ below, and our own position is not complicated: every home we list accepts vouchers.

When income falls short: co-signers, guarantor services and larger deposits

Three real options, in the order landlords usually accept them.

A co-signer. Someone with verifiable income signs alongside you and takes on the rent obligation. Ask whether the obligation is joint and several before anyone signs. See using a co-signer to rent in Ohio.

A guarantor service. A company guarantees the rent for a fee. No Ohio law requires a landlord to accept one, so confirm acceptance before paying anything — see guarantor services for renting in Ohio.

A larger security deposit. Ohio Revised Code 5321.16 does not set a dollar cap on a security deposit, and we will not print one that does not exist. What it does say is worth knowing before you offer extra money: on any deposit above the greater of fifty dollars or one month's periodic rent, the landlord owes interest on the excess at five per cent per annum once you have stayed six months or more, computed and paid to you annually (ORC 5321.16). Deductions must be itemized and identified by the landlord in a written notice, and the balance returned within thirty days after termination and delivery of possession — provided you give the landlord a forwarding address in writing, which the statute requires of the tenant.

Income verification letter you can copy and hand to your employer

Most employers will sign a letter; few want to write one. Hand them this, filled in, on their letterhead:

[Company letterhead — business name, address, phone]

[Date]

Re: Income verification for [Your Full Legal Name]

To whom it may concern:

This letter confirms that [Your Full Legal Name] has been employed by [Company Name] since [Start Date] in the position of [Job Title].

[He/She/They] currently [earn(s) $____ per hour and works an average of ____ hours per week / receives an annual salary of $____], for gross earnings of approximately $____ per month. Pay is issued [weekly / biweekly / semimonthly] by [check / direct deposit / cash].

This position is ongoing and, to the best of my knowledge, [his/her/their] employment is expected to continue.

Please contact me directly at [direct phone number] or [work email] to verify any of the above.

Sincerely,
[Signature]
[Printed Name], [Title]
[Company Name]

Before you send it, check three things: the monthly gross figure matches what your bank statements show, the phone number is a line that gets answered by a person, and the signer title is senior enough to be plausible. Those three details are what a verifier checks, and getting them right is usually the whole difference between a two-day approval and a week of silence.

Frequently asked questions

Can a landlord in Cleveland refuse to count my Social Security or disability income?
We found no Ohio statute listing which income types a private landlord must count. What is clearly prohibited is applying different standards because of a protected class: 24 CFR 100.60 makes it unlawful to use different income standards, application requirements or credit analysis because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status or national origin, and disability is protected under ORC 4112.02 as well. A landlord who counts wage income but discounts disability income is on thin ice. Rent Finder Cleveland counts verifiable benefit income.
Is source of income a protected class in Cleveland?
Not under Ohio state law. The classes listed in ORC 4112.02 are race, color, religion, sex, military status, familial status, ancestry, disability and national origin — source of income is not among them, which means an owner in Ohio can lawfully decline a voucher. Whether the particular city you are applying in has its own ordinance is a separate question, and our page on source of income discrimination in Cleveland, Ohio covers it. Wisconsin is not a different answer so much as an unresolved one. Lawful source of income does appear among the protected classes in Wis. Stat. 106.50(1m)(nm), but the statute never defines the term. The Equal Rights Division rule (Wis. Admin. Code DWD 220.02(8)) defines it openly — it includes, but is not limited to, any negotiable draft, coupon or voucher representing monetary value — yet its only example is food stamps and it never names Housing Choice Vouchers. Meanwhile an annotation printed with the statute points to Knapp v. Eagle Property Management Corp., 54 F.3d 1272 (1995), summarized there as holding that federal rent vouchers are not clearly within the meaning of the term, and the state publishes both positions side by side without reconciling them. So nobody can honestly tell you that a private landlord in Wisconsin may refuse a voucher, or that one may not; for a real case, ask a housing attorney licensed in Wisconsin. Our own policy does not depend on any of it: we accept Housing Choice Vouchers in every home we list, in Milwaukee and in every market where we operate.
How many months of bank statements do landlords ask for?
Three months is the common request and is usually enough to show a pattern. No Ohio law sets the number, so it is landlord policy. Send complete PDF statements downloaded from your bank rather than app screenshots, because a screenshot cannot be verified. If your work is seasonal, send twelve months instead and say so in one sentence.
Does the 3x rent rule apply if I have a Section 8 voucher?
The 3x rule is a market convention, not an Ohio law, and landlords who use it commonly apply it to the tenant-paid portion rather than the full rent when a voucher covers the rest. Federally, at initial occupancy where gross rent exceeds the payment standard, the family share must not exceed 40 percent of the family's adjusted monthly income under 24 CFR 982.508, and CMHA states a family generally pays approximately 30 percent of adjusted monthly income toward rent and utilities.
What if I just started a new job and have no pay stubs yet?
Use a signed offer letter on company letterhead stating your start date, rate of pay and expected hours, plus a supervisor name and a phone number that will be answered. Adding the final stubs from your previous job helps, because together they show continuous income rather than a gap.
