Where to Get Emergency Rent and Deposit Help in Cleveland (Who to Call, in Order)

Call 2-1-1 first — Cuyahoga County points to it as free, confidential referral, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. Then apply to the county’s PRC program, which covers rent, security deposits and utilities for households at or below 200% of the Federal Poverty Level, up to $1,500 a year, decided within 10 calendar days of a complete file, and paid directly to your landlord. If you already have a court date, Cleveland’s Pay to Stay ordinance lets an emergency rental assistance voucher count as your tender. PRC information line: (216) 987-7392.

Updated August 8, 2026 ·9 min read ·By the Rent Finder Cleveland team

Before you call: the documents and numbers that speed every application

Every rent-assistance program in Cuyahoga County runs on the same clock, and it is not the clock you think. The county’s own PRC standard is that an application “will be approved or denied as quickly as possible, but no later than 10 calendar days once all required verifications are received.” The ten days do not start when you apply. They start when your file is complete. Two weeks of chasing a missing pay stub is two weeks added to the front of that.

So assemble this before you dial anyone:

  • Your lease, including the page with rent amount and the utilities clause.
  • Whatever the landlord served you — a three-day notice, a pay-or-quit letter, an eviction summons. The document type changes which door you use.
  • Proof of income for every adult in the household, and proof of the loss if there was one.
  • A ledger or statement showing the exact amount owed, broken into rent and late fees. Programs pay a number, not a story.
  • Photo ID, Social Security numbers and dates of birth for everyone in the household.
  • Your landlord’s legal payee name, mailing address and phone. This one gets skipped constantly and it stalls files, because county PRC payments go to the vendor, not to you.

That last point is worth sitting with: “All payments are made directly to the vendor.” If your landlord will not provide a W-9-ready payee name and address, the money cannot move. Ask for it in writing the same day you learn you are short.

Start here: United Way of Greater Cleveland 2-1-1

Cuyahoga County’s Health and Human Services housing resources page routes residents to United Way 211, which “provides free and confidential referral services 24 hours a day, 365 days a year.” Dial 2-1-1.

Two reasons it belongs first even though it does not write checks. Funding in Cuyahoga County moves — pots open, exhaust and reopen on schedules no published page keeps current — and 2-1-1 is the only source that tracks which door is actually open this week. And an intake conversation there generates the referral that several agencies want before they will schedule you.

Be specific on the call. “I need rent help” produces a list. “I am $840 behind, I have a court date on the 19th, my landlord will accept payment, and I am in Cuyahoga County” produces a routing.

Cuyahoga County Job and Family Services: the PRC program for rent and deposits

PRC — Prevention, Retention and Contingency — is the county’s workhorse, and it is broader than most people realize. The county describes it as covering “shelter assistance services for rental assistance and/or security deposits/or utility assistance.” Rent, deposit and utilities from one application.

Note the 200% threshold. It is higher than the 175% used by HEAP and the Winter Crisis Program, so a household that was told it earns too much for energy assistance can still be inside PRC.

Note also that $1,500 is a ceiling across the year, not per event. If you use $700 on a security deposit in March, you have $800 left for a rent emergency in November. Spend it deliberately.

CHN Housing Partners and the county eviction diversion pipeline

If you call 2-1-1 in Cuyahoga County about rent, CHN Housing Partners is one of the names you are likely to be given, usually in connection with eviction diversion work tied to the county and the housing court.

We are not going to print program terms for it, and you should be suspicious of pages that do. We checked the county’s financial-assistance pages and the city’s housing pages, and no primary government source we could open publishes CHN’s current program names, income limits, maximum awards or turnaround times. The county page that would carry them timed out repeatedly. Amounts you find on aggregator sites are frequently pandemic-era figures for programs that have since closed.

What to do instead: treat the referral as real and the numbers as unknown. When you reach intake, ask four questions and write the answers down — Is this program currently funded and open? What is the maximum? Do you pay arrears, forward rent, or both? What is your decision turnaround? Those four answers tell you whether to keep waiting or to file PRC in parallel. Filing in parallel is usually right; nothing in the county’s published PRC criteria requires you to wait for another agency to finish.

EDEN, Inc. and coordinated entry for households facing homelessness

The same honesty applies to EDEN, Inc. It is a name that circulates constantly in Cleveland housing conversations, particularly around coordinated entry and permanent supportive housing, and we could not verify its current program terms, eligibility or amounts on any primary government source.

The distinction worth understanding is between prevention and coordinated entry. Prevention money keeps you in a unit you already hold — that is PRC territory, and it is where you belong if you have a lease and a balance. Coordinated entry is the intake system for households that are already homeless or imminently will be, and it assesses and prioritizes rather than cutting a check on request. If you still have a unit, do not let a coordinated-entry referral pull you off the prevention track; run both.

If your situation is same-day rather than same-month, our page on same-day emergency housing in Cleveland is the faster read.

If you already have a court date: Cleveland’s Right to Counsel and Legal Aid Society intake

Cleveland legislated a right to counsel in eviction cases, and the City of Cleveland names the Legal Aid Society — alongside the Northeast Ohio Coalition for the Homeless — as where tenants facing eviction should turn. Eligibility for the right-to-counsel program is limited and determined at intake. We could not open a current City of Cleveland page stating today’s criteria, so we are not going to guess at them here: call 2-1-1 for the routing rather than deciding on your own that you do not qualify.

Two things that are on the record and matter more than most people realize.

Cleveland’s Pay to Stay ordinance. The City’s description: if a tenant is summoned to court for an eviction, they can stop the proceeding if they have the full amount of rent owed plus any late fees. And the provision that changes the sequencing of everything above — the tender may include “emergency rental assistance vouchers from an accredited social service, nonprofit, governmental, or quasi-governmental agency.” An approval letter can function as your tender. That is exactly why you tell the agency your hearing date on the first call, and why a pending PRC decision is worth mentioning in court rather than sitting on.

Where the case is heard. Cleveland Housing Court is at 1200 Ontario Street, 13B, Cleveland, Ohio 44113, open Monday through Friday, 9 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. Go early, and go before your hearing date if you can — a full understanding of the Ohio eviction timeline is worth having before you walk in.

Deposit and first-month help for voucher holders

A Housing Choice Voucher covers a share of rent. It does not cover the security deposit, and that gap is the most common reason a voucher holder with an approved unit still cannot move. Three levers exist:

  • PRC covers security deposits outright, subject to the 200% FPL test and the $1,500 annual cap, paid directly to the landlord.
  • Winter Crisis covers the utility side of a move — its eligible situations expressly include households that “need to establish new services” or “need to pay to transfer service,” with a maximum benefit of $1,200 between November 1 and March 31. Utility deposits are a real move-in cost and this is the program built for them.
  • The Special Reconnect Order lets any residential customer of a PUCO-regulated electric or gas company, regardless of income, pay $175 to establish service instead of a full security deposit, once per winter heating season. It does not apply to Cleveland Public Power, which is municipal and outside PUCO’s jurisdiction.

Our page on deposit and move-in assistance with a voucher in Cleveland goes deeper, and how to apply for Section 8 through CMHA covers the voucher itself.

One legal point to bank for later: under Ohio Revised Code §5321.16, a deposit above $50 or one month’s rent — whichever is greater — earns 5% annual interest on the excess if you stay six months or more, and any deduction must be itemized in writing and delivered with the balance “within thirty days after termination of the rental agreement and delivery of possession.” You have to give the landlord a forwarding address in writing. Miss that and you lose the damages and attorney-fee remedy. See our full Ohio security deposit law guide.

Faith-based and neighborhood funds that move fastest

Small parish, mosque and neighborhood-association funds are frequently the fastest money in Cleveland — sometimes days rather than weeks — because they are discretionary rather than rule-bound. They are also the hardest category to publish accurately: amounts are small, funds run dry mid-month, and no government source maintains a list.

Rather than name organizations whose current capacity we cannot verify, use the mechanism that finds them: 2-1-1 maintains the live inventory, and it is the only reason to call the same number twice in a month. Ask specifically for “one-time emergency assistance funds accepting applications this week.”

Two habits make these work. Ask for a partial amount rather than the whole balance — small funds say yes to $300 far more often than to $900, and three yeses beat one no. And ask each one whether they issue an approval letter or voucher, because under Pay to Stay that paper is what you carry into court.

What to do while you wait: written payment plan, escrow, and keeping the case out of court

Assume every application above takes longer than promised, and work the landlord relationship in parallel.

Get the payment plan in writing. Email is fine and email is better than a phone call, because it creates the record. State the amount, the dates, and the fact that an assistance application is pending with an agency name. Most Cleveland landlords would rather have a documented plan than an eviction filing, which costs them money and an occupied month.

Understand what escrow is and is not. Ohio Revised Code §5321.07 lets a tenant deposit rent with the clerk of the municipal or county court — but only after giving the landlord written notice of a condition the landlord failed to remedy, and only, in the statute’s words, “if the tenant is current in rent payments due under the rental agreement.” Escrow is a repair remedy, not a nonpayment remedy. If you are behind on rent, you cannot escrow your way out of it, and trying will make the case worse. The section also does not apply where the landlord is party to rental agreements covering three or fewer dwelling units and gave written notice of that fact.

Do not go silent. The single most damaging thing a tenant does in this window is stop responding. Silence reads as abandonment and it removes the landlord’s reason to wait on your pending approval.

And if the honest arithmetic says this unit is out of reach even after assistance, the fastest exit from the cycle is a lower rent. That is the one part of this we can actually help with — see the low-income housing help overview, then call us.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I get emergency rent assistance in Cleveland?
Start with two calls. United Way 2-1-1, which Cuyahoga County describes as providing free and confidential referral services 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, and the county’s Prevention, Retention and Contingency (PRC) program, which covers rental assistance and/or security deposits and/or utility assistance for households at or below 200% of the Federal Poverty Level, up to $1,500 per year. The PRC information line is (216) 987-7392.
Does Cuyahoga County help with a security deposit?
Yes. Cuyahoga County’s PRC program explicitly lists security deposits alongside rental assistance and utility assistance. The income test is 200% of the Federal Poverty Level and the maximum is $1,500 per year across all shelter assistance. Payments go directly to the vendor — your landlord or the utility — not to you.
Can I get a free lawyer for an eviction in Cleveland?
Cleveland legislated a right to counsel in eviction cases, and the City names the Legal Aid Society as a source of help for tenants. Eligibility is limited and determined at intake, and we could not verify the current criteria on a City of Cleveland page that resolves today, so do not assume you qualify or that you do not. Call 2-1-1 to be routed to intake, and call before your hearing date rather than the morning of.
How fast can rent assistance be approved in Cuyahoga County?
The county’s published standard is that a PRC application will be approved or denied as quickly as possible, but no later than 10 calendar days once all required verifications are received. The clock starts when your file is complete, not when you first apply, which is why gathering documents before you call is the single biggest thing you control. The PRC information line asks you to leave a message and allow 48 hours for a response.
Can I get rent help if I have a Section 8 voucher?
The county’s published PRC criteria are income-based — at or below 200% of the Federal Poverty Level — and do not list voucher status as a disqualifier, so ask at intake rather than assuming. Assistance would go toward the portion of rent you owe, not the housing authority’s portion. Separately, the Winter Crisis Program covers the cost of establishing new utility service or transferring service, which is often the real barrier for a voucher holder trying to move.
