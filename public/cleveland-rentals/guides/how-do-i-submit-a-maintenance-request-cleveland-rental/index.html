How to Submit a Maintenance Request on Your Cleveland Rental

File it in the resident portal so it is timestamped, and call the after-hours line on top of that if it is an emergency — no heat, no running water, an active leak, sewage, or anything touching electrical. Ohio law does not define "emergency," but it does define the landlord's duties (ORC §5321.04) and the tenant's remedies (ORC §5321.07). The remedies only unlock after you give written notice, sent "to the person or place where rent is normally paid," and only if you are current in rent. After that, the landlord has a reasonable time given the severity, or 30 days, whichever is sooner.

Where to file: portal, text and the after-hours line

Use the resident portal first, every time, even when you have already called. A portal ticket does three things a phone call cannot: it timestamps the report, it attaches your photos to the unit record, and it survives staff turnover. If a dispute ever reaches a courtroom or a code inspector, the ticket history is the evidence.

Then escalate by phone if the condition is urgent. A text message to a leasing agent's cell is the least reliable channel in property management — it is on one person's device, it is not attached to your unit, and it is invisible to the technician being dispatched. Use it to say "I filed ticket #1042, it's the water heater," not to make the request.

For anything you may later need to enforce, there is a fourth channel that is legally distinct: the written notice under ORC §5321.07. The statute is specific about where it goes — the notice "shall be sent to the person or place where rent is normally paid." That is the address on your ledger, not the maintenance inbox. More on that below.

What counts as an emergency in a Cleveland rental (and what does not)

There is no statutory definition of "emergency" anywhere in Ohio's Chapter 5321. What exists instead is a list of landlord duties, and the ones written without qualification are the ones that behave like emergencies in practice. ORC §5321.04(A) requires the landlord to:

  • "Make all repairs and do whatever is reasonably necessary to put and keep the premises in a fit and habitable condition";
  • "Maintain in good and safe working order and condition all electrical, plumbing, sanitary, heating, ventilating, and air conditioning fixtures and appliances, and elevators, supplied or required to be supplied by the landlord";
  • "Supply running water, reasonable amounts of hot water, and reasonable heat at all times," except where the building is not required by law to be equipped for that purpose or where heat or hot water is generated by an installation in the tenant's exclusive control on a direct utility connection;
  • "Keep all common areas of the premises in a safe and sanitary condition" — the shared stair, porch and basement of a Cleveland double;
  • Provide and maintain waste receptacles and arrange removal, but only "when the landlord is a party to any rental agreements that cover four or more dwelling units in the same structure."

Call the emergency line for: no heat between roughly November and April, no running water, no hot water, an active leak, water reaching a panel or fixture, a gas smell, sewage backup, a non-locking exterior door, or a total power loss inside the unit when the neighbors have power.

File a normal ticket for: a dripping faucet, one dead outlet, a slow drain, a loose railing, a running toilet, an appliance that works badly rather than not at all, or a window that sticks. These are real and they get fixed — they just do not need a 2 a.m. dispatch, and calling them in as emergencies makes the genuine ones slower for everyone in the portfolio.

One Cleveland-specific note: heat is the request that spikes here, and it spikes on the first cold snap when a furnace that idled all summer fails on its first hard run. Filing a "furnace runs but doesn't heat well" ticket in October is the difference between a scheduled visit and a no-heat emergency in January. What to write: the five details that get a technician out faster

A ticket that reads "kitchen sink broken" produces a truck roll with the wrong parts. Give the dispatcher enough to send the right trade with the right van:

  • Exact location. Which room, which fixture, which floor. "Second-floor rear bathroom, the tub, not the sink."
  • What it does and when it started. "Started Saturday. No hot water at all; cold works fine." A symptom plus a date beats an adjective.
  • What you already tried. Reset the breaker, checked the GFCI, cleared the trap. This eliminates the first twenty minutes of the visit.
  • Access details. Dog in the kitchen, basement door locked, gate code, best entry. Say plainly whether the technician may enter when nobody is home.
  • Availability windows. Two real windows in the next three business days beats "anytime," which in practice means the slot nobody else wanted.

If the condition affects heat, water, sewage or electrical, say so explicitly in the first line. Tickets get triaged on the first sentence.

Photos and video: how to document a repair so nobody argues later

Photograph before anyone touches it. Three shots: wide (which room), medium (the fixture), tight (the actual defect), plus ten seconds of video with sound if the problem is a noise, a drip rate or a burner that will not light. Note the date — phone metadata does this for you as long as you do not screenshot and re-save.

Then keep photographing after. A repair that leaves a stained ceiling or a cut-open drywall patch is a repair with a second half, and the "after" photo is what gets that second half scheduled. This is also the same discipline that protects your deposit: compare your ticket photos to your move-in condition report, and read our guides on the move-in inspection checklist and normal wear and tear in an Ohio rental so that a maintenance ticket does not quietly become a move-out charge.

Access and the 24-hour entry rule under Ohio law

Ohio's entry rule is one of the most misquoted lines in the chapter, so here it is exactly. ORC §5321.04(A)(8) requires the landlord, "except in the case of emergency or if it is impracticable to do so," to "give the tenant reasonable notice of the landlord's intent to enter and enter only at reasonable times," and states that "twenty-four hours is presumed to be a reasonable notice in the absence of evidence to the contrary."

That is a presumption, not a fixed rule — and it is expressly displaced in an emergency. If you report a burst pipe at midnight, you have effectively requested entry.

The obligation runs both ways. ORC §5321.05(B) provides that the tenant "shall not unreasonably withhold consent for the landlord to enter into the dwelling unit in order to inspect the premises, make ordinary, necessary, or agreed repairs." Repeatedly declining access is the fastest way to make a repair take months, and it undercuts a later complaint that the repair was never made.

If a landlord does enter improperly, §5321.04(B) gives the tenant a remedy: actual damages resulting from the entry or demands, injunctive relief to prevent recurrence, and a judgment for reasonable attorney's fees — or the right to terminate the rental agreement. The same subsection covers "repeated demands for entry otherwise lawful that have the effect of harassing the tenant."

Where the ticket ends and the statute begins

The statutory clock does not start when you file a ticket. It starts when you give written notice. Section 5321.07(A) says the tenant "may give notice in writing to the landlord, specifying the acts, omissions, or code violations that constitute noncompliance," and that "the notice shall be sent to the person or place where rent is normally paid." From there §5321.07(B) measures the landlord against "a reasonable time considering the severity of the condition and the time necessary to remedy it, or within thirty days, whichever is sooner," and opens the three remedies — deposit rent with the clerk of court, apply to the court for an order directing the landlord to remedy the condition, or terminate the rental agreement — only if the tenant "is current in rent payments due under the rental agreement."

That clock has its own page, and it is the one to read next. How to word the notice so it counts, why thirty days is a ceiling rather than a target, and the situations in §5321.07(C) and (D) that switch the whole section off are covered in how long a landlord has to make repairs in Ohio. The mechanics of depositing rent are in our Ohio rent escrow guide, and the full duty list on both sides is in Ohio tenant rights on repairs. This page stays on the part you control: filing the ticket so it counts.

Repairs you are responsible for as the tenant under ORC §5321.05

Chapter 5321 assigns the tenant a real list, and knowing it keeps chargebacks off your ledger. Under §5321.05(A) the tenant must "keep that part of the premises that he occupies and uses safe and sanitary," dispose of waste properly, keep plumbing fixtures as clean as their condition permits, "use and operate all electrical and plumbing fixtures properly," comply with applicable housing, health and safety codes, and personally refrain — and forbid guests — from "intentionally or negligently destroying, defacing, damaging, or removing any fixture, appliance, or other part of the premises."

One clause is worth reading twice: §5321.05(A)(7) requires the tenant to maintain in good working order the range, refrigerator, washer, dryer, dishwasher or other appliances supplied by the landlord — but only where they are "required to be maintained by the tenant under the terms and conditions of a written rental agreement." No written clause, no tenant obligation for the landlord's appliances. Conversely, ORC §5321.13(F) allows the landlord to agree to assume duties the statute would otherwise put on the tenant.

Where the tenant does cause damage, §5321.05(C)(1) lets the landlord "recover any actual damages that result from the violation together with reasonable attorney's fees" — a rare place where attorney's fees are available in this chapter, since §5321.13(C) refuses to enforce a lease clause promising them.

If nothing happens: written notice, rent escrow and Cleveland's code complaint line

Escalate in this order, and keep copies of each step.

  • Send the §5321.07 written notice to the place where rent is normally paid. Certified mail with return receipt, or email plus a mailed copy. Name the condition, the date first reported, and the ticket number.
  • Stay current in rent. The remedies are conditioned on it. Depositing rent with the clerk is a payment, not a withholding.
  • Call the City. Cleveland's Department of Building & Housing takes code complaints at (216) 664-2282 and is located at 601 Lakeside Ave, Room 510, Cleveland, OH 44114; the City also routes reports through its 311 service. A governmental finding matters legally: §5321.07(A) treats a finding by a governmental agency that the premises are not in compliance with building, housing, health or safety codes affecting health and safety as an independent trigger for the tenant's remedies.
  • Ask about lead if the home predates 1978. The City states that "rental properties built before 1978 must obtain a Lead Safe Certification or an Exemption." Most of Cleveland's rental stock is that old — see what Lead Safe Certification means for renters.

Ohio also forbids retaliation for using these channels. ORC §5321.02 provides that "a landlord may not retaliate against a tenant by increasing the tenant's rent, decreasing services that are due to the tenant, or bringing or threatening to bring an action for possession of the tenant's premises" because the tenant complained to a governmental agency, complained to the landlord about a §5321.04 duty, or joined with other tenants.

Section 8 units: how a failed inspection item becomes a work order

On a voucher unit, a maintenance problem has a second track with hard federal deadlines. Under 24 CFR §982.405, when a tenant reports a deficiency: "if the reported deficiency is life-threatening, the PHA must, within 24 hours of notification, both inspect the housing unit and notify the owner if the life-threatening deficiency is confirmed. The owner must then make the repairs within 24 hours of PHA notification." For a non-life-threatening deficiency, "the PHA must, within 15 days of notification, both inspect the unit and notify the owner if the deficiency is confirmed. The owner must then make the repairs within 30 days of notification from the PHA or within any PHA-approved extension."

The enforcement lever is money. 24 CFR §982.404(a) puts the duty on the owner — "the owner must maintain the unit in accordance with HQS" — and sets the windows: "If a deficiency is life-threatening, the owner must correct the deficiency within 24 hours of notification. For other deficiencies, the owner must correct the deficiency within 30 calendar days of notification (or any reasonable PHA-approved extension)." When the owner misses that window, §982.404(d) lets the PHA withhold the housing assistance payment after notifying the owner in writing, and requires the PHA to abate it if the repairs still are not made. Abatement carries a warning to family and owner that if the unit does not meet HQS within 60 days of the determination of noncompliance, "the PHA will terminate the HAP contract for the unit, and the family will have to move if the family wishes to receive continued assistance" — with the voucher to move issued at least 30 days before that termination. One date matters when you read this section: §982.404(f) applies this version to HAP contracts "executed on or after or renewed after June 6, 2024," and leaves older contracts on the previous version.

The same regulation puts some items on the family. §982.404(b) makes the family responsible for an HQS breach caused by failing to pay tenant-paid utilities, failing to provide and maintain tenant-supplied appliances, or where "any member of the household or guest damages the dwelling unit or premises (damages beyond ordinary wear and tear)." Read the trigger carefully, because it is narrower than it looks: the 24-hour and 30-day repair clocks land on the family only where the PHA has used §982.404(a)(4) to waive the owner's responsibility for a deficiency it determined the household or a guest caused. Without that waiver the repair stays the owner's job. Section 982.405 also requires the PHA to inspect "at least biennially during assisted occupancy" — once every three years for a small rural PHA — and provides that "the PHA may not charge the family for an initial inspection or reinspection of the unit."

Frequently asked questions

What is considered an emergency repair in a rental?
Ohio's landlord-tenant chapter contains no statutory definition of “emergency,” so the working definition comes from the landlord duties in ORC §5321.04 and from your lease. The duties most likely to be treated as emergencies are the ones the statute states without qualification: the landlord must “supply running water, reasonable amounts of hot water, and reasonable heat at all times,” must “maintain in good and safe working order and condition all electrical, plumbing, sanitary, heating, ventilating, and air conditioning fixtures and appliances” it supplies, and must “make all repairs and do whatever is reasonably necessary to put and keep the premises in a fit and habitable condition.” No heat in a Cleveland January, no running water, an active leak reaching electrical, or a sewage backup are the classic calls. For a voucher unit, 24 CFR §982.404(a)(3) sets a hard rule: “If a deficiency is life-threatening, the owner must correct the deficiency within 24 hours of notification.”
Can I withhold rent in Ohio until a repair is made?
No — depositing rent with the court is not the same thing as not paying it. ORC §5321.07(B)(1) lets a qualifying tenant “deposit all rent that is due and thereafter becomes due the landlord with the clerk of the municipal or county court having jurisdiction in the territory in which the residential premises are located.” The money still gets paid; it is paid to the clerk instead of the landlord. You must have given written notice first and you must be current in rent. There is also an exception: ORC §5321.07(C) says the section does not apply to a landlord who is party to rental agreements covering three or fewer dwelling units and who gives written notice of that fact.
Does my landlord need to give notice before coming to make a repair?
Yes, except in an emergency or where it is impracticable. ORC §5321.04(A)(8) requires the landlord to “give the tenant reasonable notice of the landlord's intent to enter and enter only at reasonable times,” and adds that “twenty-four hours is presumed to be a reasonable notice in the absence of evidence to the contrary.” On the other side, ORC §5321.05(B) says the tenant “shall not unreasonably withhold consent for the landlord to enter into the dwelling unit in order to inspect the premises, make ordinary, necessary, or agreed repairs.” If a landlord enters in violation of the notice rule, ORC §5321.04(B) gives the tenant actual damages, injunctive relief and attorney's fees, or the right to terminate.
Who pays if I caused the damage?
You generally do. ORC §5321.05 requires the tenant to keep the occupied part of the premises safe and sanitary, to “use and operate all electrical and plumbing fixtures properly,” and to refrain from and forbid others from “intentionally or negligently destroying, defacing, damaging, or removing any fixture, appliance, or other part of the premises.” Under ORC §5321.05(C)(1), a landlord may recover actual damages resulting from a violation together with reasonable attorney's fees. In a voucher unit, 24 CFR §982.404(b) reaches the same result: the family may be held responsible for an HQS breach where “any member of the household or guest damages the dwelling unit or premises (damages beyond ordinary wear and tear)” — but the repair clock only moves to the family where the PHA has waived the owner’s responsibility for that deficiency under §982.404(a)(4).
