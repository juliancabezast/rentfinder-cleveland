What Landlords Check on an Ohio Rental Application (and What Shows Up)

An Ohio rental screening is a consumer report assembled by a third-party company: credit file, court records (evictions, civil judgments, criminal history), identity verification, and whatever income and landlord references the application asks you to supply. The federal Fair Credit Reporting Act sets how far back it may reach — bankruptcies drop off after 10 years, and civil suits, judgments, arrest records and collection accounts after seven, with criminal convictions carved out of that seven-year limit. Ohio adds no shorter lookback. What Ohio and federal fair housing law do require is that whatever standard a landlord uses gets applied the same way to every applicant.

Who runs the report and what law governs it

The landlord almost never assembles your file personally. A tenant-screening company — a consumer reporting agency in the language of the statute — pulls a credit file from one of the nationwide bureaus, runs a name-and-date-of-birth search against court records, and returns a packet. Ohio publishes no statutory cap on what an application fee may be, and we are not going to print a typical range as though it were a rule — the number you are quoted comes from that landlord's own policy, not from the Revised Code. Ask what the fee covers and whether any part of it is refundable before you hand it over.

The governing statute is the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. §1681 and following. It lets a landlord obtain a report on you because the landlord "otherwise has a legitimate business need for the information … in connection with a business transaction that is initiated by the consumer" — you applied, so the permissible purpose exists (15 U.S.C. §1681b). That same statute is what limits the report's contents, gives you the right to a copy, and gives you the right to dispute it.

Worth knowing if you are comparing markets: Wisconsin caps this. Under Wis. Stat. §704.085, a Milwaukee landlord "may require a prospective tenant to pay the landlord's actual cost, up to $25, to obtain a consumer credit report," must tell you about the charge before it is incurred, and may not charge you at all if you hand over your own nationwide-bureau report that is less than 30 days old. Ohio has no equivalent provision.

Credit: what a landlord sees, and what a thin file looks like

A rental credit pull typically surfaces four things: open tradelines and their balances, payment history, collection accounts, and public-record items the bureau still carries. In the $700–$1,800 rent band that dominates Cleveland, Akron, Lorain and Elyria, most owners are not reading a three-digit score in isolation. They are looking for two specific signals — an open utility or telecom collection (because it predicts a service shutoff at the new address) and a balance owed to a prior landlord.

No Ohio statute sets a minimum credit score for renting. The number in a landlord's criteria sheet is that landlord's own policy. The federal rule that does bite is 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4), which makes it unlawful to use "different qualification criteria or applications … such as income standards, application requirements, application fees, credit analysis or sale or rental approval procedures" because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status or national origin. A landlord may set the bar wherever it likes; it may not move the bar between applicants for those reasons.

A thin file — no score, or a score the bureau declines to generate — is common among applicants who have never financed a car or carried a credit card. It is not a derogatory file, and it is worth saying so in writing on the application. Twelve months of paid rent receipts, a utility account in your own name, and a bank statement showing consistent deposits do more work than an explanation. Eviction records in Ohio: where they come from and how long they stay

Screening companies do not have a magic eviction database. They read court dockets. In the city of Cleveland that docket has a specific home: ORC §1901.011 states that "there is hereby created a housing division in the Cleveland municipal court and in the Toledo municipal court." Elsewhere in the region — Lorain, Elyria, Akron, the Cuyahoga County suburbs — forcible entry and detainer cases run through the ordinary municipal court for that jurisdiction. This matters practically: a Cleveland filing and an Akron filing land in different dockets, and a screener that only searches one county can miss or duplicate a record.

The entry is created when the case is filed, not when it is decided. Ohio requires the landlord to serve a notice "three or more days before beginning the action" (ORC §1923.04) — but once the complaint is filed, the public record exists whether you moved out, paid, settled, or won. That is why a dismissed case still turns up.

How long may it be reported? Under 15 U.S.C. §1681c, a consumer report may not contain "civil suits, civil judgments, and records of arrest that … antedate the report by more than seven years or until the governing statute of limitations has expired, whichever is the longer period." A money judgment from an eviction is a civil judgment and falls under that clause. If you have a filing in your past, read our full walkthrough on renting with an eviction on your record in Ohio before you apply anywhere.

Criminal history: the seven-year rule and what happened to HUD's 2016 guidance on blanket bans

Two things are true at once here, and most articles on the internet get the second one wrong.

First, the FCRA time limits. Section 1681c bars a report from containing "any other adverse item of information, other than records of convictions of crimes which antedates the report by more than seven years." Read that carefully: arrest records are explicitly named in the seven-year clause, but convictions are carved out of it. There is no federal age cutoff on reporting a criminal conviction in a tenant screening report.

Second, the fair housing overlay. For nearly a decade the standard reference was HUD's Office of General Counsel guidance of April 4, 2016 on the use of criminal records. That document is no longer current. A memorandum from the HUD General Counsel on fair housing guidance memoranda and documents — published by HUD under the file name OGC-Memo-on-Fair-Housing-Guidance-9-25-2025, though the document itself carries no date line — lists "OGC Guidance Application of Fair Housing Act Standards to Use of Criminal Records" (April 4, 2016) among the documents that "no longer express the official legal opinion and/or official policies and procedures of the Office of General Counsel," adding that "updated guidance related to fair housing matters from the Office of General Counsel will be issued at the appropriate time." If you are reading a 2023 blog post that quotes the 2016 guidance as live HUD policy, it is out of date.

What did not change: the Fair Housing Act itself, Ohio's ORC §4112.02(H), and 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4)'s prohibition on applying different criteria by protected class. A criminal-record policy still has to be the same policy for everyone. If a record is what is standing between you and a lease, the practical move is to look for owners who publish a criteria sheet and to check our second-chance houses for rent in Cleveland.

Income and employment verification

Income verification is the part of screening the applicant actually controls. Most Cleveland-area owners ask for the last 30 days of pay stubs, or two to three months of bank statements for self-employed and gig income, plus an employer contact. Benefit income — Social Security, SSI, SSDI, pension, child support — is verified from the award letter or the deposit history rather than a stub.

There is no Ohio statute establishing an income multiple. "Three times the rent" is market practice, not law, and it is applied to gross monthly income by most owners who use it. What is legally binding is 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4): the income standard has to be the same standard for every applicant. A landlord that quietly requires 3x from one household and 2.5x from another, for a reason tied to a protected characteristic, has a fair housing problem regardless of how the arithmetic comes out.

For voucher holders, the arithmetic is different by design. Under 24 CFR §982.508, when a PHA approves a tenancy for initial occupancy and the gross rent exceeds the payment standard, "the family share must not exceed 40 percent of the family's adjusted monthly income." CMHA states the same limit for its own new contracts: the total tenant portion paid directly to the owner "can be no more than 40% of the family's monthly income." That cap is enforced by CMHA at lease-up, before the owner ever sees the file.

Landlord references: the three questions previous landlords are asked

Reference calls in this market are short. In practice a leasing manager asks three questions, and the value of the answer drops sharply after them:

  • What were the exact move-in and move-out dates? This is really an identity and consistency check against what you wrote on the application. Mismatched dates are the single most common reason a file stalls.
  • Was rent paid in full and on time, and is there a balance owed? A balance owed to a prior landlord is the item most likely to be treated as disqualifying, because it is concrete and collectible.
  • Was proper notice given, and would you rent to this household again? The notice half of that question is why the 30-day rule in ORC §5321.17 matters twice — once when you leave, and again on your next application.

One structural warning specific to a market with many small owners: your "previous landlord" may be the person who benefits from you not leaving, or the person you left on bad terms. Bring documentation that does not depend on their goodwill — cancelled checks, a payment ledger printout, the itemized deposit statement Ohio requires under ORC §5321.16, which says "any deduction from the security deposit shall be itemized and identified by the landlord in a written notice."

Written screening criteria: why every applicant should be measured the same way

Ask for the criteria in writing before you pay a fee. An owner that can hand you a one-page standard — minimum income, how collections are treated, how old an eviction filing has to be, what documents substitute for a credit score — is an owner who can also demonstrate that the same sheet was used on the applicant before you. That is exactly the evidence 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4) contemplates.

It also saves you money. Application fees in Ohio are not refundable by statute and not capped by statute, so the cheapest way to avoid paying twice is to find out you do not meet a criterion before you apply, not after. How to read your own report before you apply

Pull your own file first. 15 U.S.C. §1681j requires the nationwide agencies to make the disclosures in §1681g "once during any 12-month period upon request of the consumer and without charge to the consumer," through the centralized source. Then read it in this order:

  • Identity block first. Names, former addresses, employers. A merged file — someone else's record attached to your name or a relative's — is the error that most often produces a denial you cannot explain.
  • Public records second. Any civil judgment or eviction-related entry: check the county, the case number, and the disposition. Cleveland Housing Division cases and suburban municipal court cases will look different on the page.
  • Collections third. Note the original creditor. Utility and telecom collections are the ones a landlord will ask you about, because service has to be turned on in your name at move-in.
  • Dates last. Anything older than seven years that is not a criminal conviction, and any bankruptcy older than 10 years, should not be there at all.

Fixing errors: disputes, sealed records and what a landlord must ignore

If something is wrong, dispute it with the reporting agency in writing and keep the receipt. Under 15 U.S.C. §1681i, the agency must "conduct a reasonable reinvestigation to determine whether the disputed information is inaccurate … or delete the item from the file … before the end of the 30-day period beginning on the date on which the agency receives the notice of the dispute." That window "may be extended for not more than 15 additional days if the consumer reporting agency receives information from the consumer during that 30-day period that is relevant to the reinvestigation" — so 45 days is the realistic outer bound if you keep sending documents.

Two things a landlord should not be seeing at all, because §1681c keeps them out of the report: a bankruptcy that antedates the report by more than 10 years, and a civil suit, civil judgment, arrest record or collection account past the seven-year line (measured, for suits and judgments, against the longer of seven years or the governing statute of limitations). If a court has sealed or otherwise restricted a record and it is still appearing on a screening report, that is an accuracy dispute under §1681i like any other — send the court's order with the dispute.

And if the denial has already happened, you are owed a specific notice with specific contents. Frequently asked questions

How far back does a rental background check go in Ohio?
The federal Fair Credit Reporting Act sets the outer limits. A consumer report may not include bankruptcies that antedate the report by more than 10 years, and civil suits, civil judgments and arrest records that antedate it by more than seven years “or until the governing statute of limitations has expired, whichever is the longer period.” Collection accounts and “any other adverse item of information” also drop off at seven years — with one carve-out: the seven-year rule applies to items “other than records of convictions of crimes.” Ohio does not add a shorter lookback of its own.
Does an eviction filing show up even if the case was dismissed?
It can. Screening companies pull the court docket, and in Cleveland eviction cases are filed in the housing division created by ORC §1901.011 — the docket entry exists from the day the case is filed, regardless of how it ended. Under the FCRA, a civil suit may be reported for seven years or until the governing statute of limitations expires, whichever is longer. If the report shows the filing but not the dismissal or a judgment in your favor, that is an accuracy problem you can dispute under 15 U.S.C. §1681i, and the agency generally has 30 days to reinvestigate.
Can a Cleveland landlord deny me for a criminal record?
Having a criminal record is not itself a protected class under Ohio's fair housing law, whose list in ORC §4112.02(H) is race, color, religion, sex, military status, familial status, ancestry, disability and national origin. What the law does require is consistency: 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4) makes it unlawful to use different qualification criteria, application requirements, application fees or credit analysis because of a protected characteristic. Note also that HUD's April 4, 2016 OGC guidance on criminal records was listed as no longer expressing the Office of General Counsel's official legal opinion in a HUD General Counsel memorandum on fair housing guidance memoranda and documents, so do not treat summaries of that 2016 document as current federal guidance.
Can I see the screening report the landlord used?
Yes, in two ways. If you are denied based on a consumer report, 15 U.S.C. §1681m requires the landlord to give you oral, written or electronic notice of the adverse action, identify the reporting agency by name, address and toll-free number, state that “the consumer reporting agency did not make the decision to take the adverse action,” and tell you about your right to a free copy of the report within 60 days. Separately, 15 U.S.C. §1681j entitles you to a free file disclosure from each nationwide agency once every 12 months through the centralized source.
Do landlords check credit for Section 8 applicants?
Landlord screening and CMHA eligibility are two separate gates. CMHA determines program eligibility under 24 CFR §982.201 — you must be a “family,” be income-eligible, and be a citizen or a noncitizen with eligible immigration status — and its preliminary application asks for an active private email account, Social Security numbers, and the names, dates of birth and income of every household member. Credit is not part of that federal eligibility test. The owner then runs its own screening, and under 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4) that screening has to be the same one applied to every other applicant.
