How Lease Renewal Works in Cleveland: Timing, Notice, and Rent Changes

Ohio sets no deadline for a renewal offer, no statutory notice period for a rent increase, and has no rent control — ORC §5321.20 even preempts local governments from regulating the rights and obligations of parties to a rental agreement. Your renewal timetable therefore comes from one place: the notice clause in your own lease. What Ohio does set is the 30-day notice to end a month-to-month tenancy under ORC §5321.17, which binds landlord and tenant equally. If you hold a Housing Choice Voucher, the owner must tell the PHA about a rent change at least 60 days before it takes effect, and the PHA must re-test rent reasonableness before any increase.

The renewal calendar: work backwards from your lease's notice clause

There is no statutory renewal calendar in Ohio. Chapter 5321 says nothing about when a renewal offer has to go out or when your answer is due, so the only date that binds you is the one printed in your own lease — the notice window it sets for declining to renew. Find that clause, put its deadline in your calendar, and treat every other date as planning rather than obligation.

Where a number does come from the statute is the periodic tenancy: ORC §5321.17 uses thirty days to end a month-to-month arrangement, and thirty days is also about what a Cleveland leasing team needs to photograph, list, show and screen a replacement household before the turn. If your lease's notice deadline is coming up and no renewal paperwork has appeared, ask for it — it is far easier to negotiate before a unit is advertised than after.

Voucher households need a longer runway, because a third party is in the loop. The 60-day federal notice on rent changes (below), plus CMHA's own processing time, is the reason to open the conversation early rather than at the deadline.

What happens automatically if you do nothing when the lease ends

Nothing in Ohio's Chapter 5321 tells you what happens when a fixed-term lease runs out and the tenant stays. That answer lives in your lease's holdover clause, which ORC §5321.06 permits: a landlord and tenant "may include in a rental agreement any terms and conditions, including any term relating to rent, the duration of an agreement, and any other provisions governing the rights and obligations of the parties that are not inconsistent with or prohibited by Chapter 5321."

Most leases in this market convert to a month-to-month tenancy — often at a higher rate, sometimes a substantially higher one. Two consequences follow immediately. First, both sides gain the ability to end things on 30 days' notice under §5321.17. Second, you lose the price certainty a term lease buys. Read the clause before you let a lease lapse by inattention; our guide to month-to-month leases in Ohio covers what changes.

Rent increases in Ohio: what governs them and what notice you get

There is no Ohio statute setting a maximum increase, and no statute setting a notice period specifically for an increase. The governing documents are your lease and, if you are periodic, §5321.17: "the landlord or the tenant may terminate or fail to renew a month-to-month tenancy by notice given the other at least thirty days prior to the periodic rental date" — seven days for week-to-week. That cuts both ways, which is the part tenants forget: the same section is what lets you leave on 30 days when the increase is one you will not pay.

Rent control is off the table statewide. ORC §5321.20, effective September 23, 2022, "preempt[s] political subdivisions from regulating the rights and obligations of parties to a rental agreement" under Chapter 5321.

So how do you tell whether an increase is reasonable? Use a published benchmark rather than a feeling. HUD's Fair Market Rents for fiscal year 2026 are an administrative standard, not an observed market average — but they are the only figure in this space that is published, regional and verifiable:

Renewing for 12 months vs going month to month

A 12-month renewal buys price certainty. Month-to-month buys flexibility and usually costs a premium, because the owner carries turnover risk every month instead of once a year. We are not going to publish a typical premium for this market, because nobody publishes one — the only figure that means anything is the one on your own renewal offer. Ask for both numbers side by side, multiply the monthly difference by twelve, and compare that annual figure against the actual cost of the move you were keeping your options open for. Month-to-month earns its keep when you have a real, dated reason: a job start, a closing, a family situation with a known timeline. The full term-by-term comparison lives in our guide to month-to-month vs 12-month leases in Ohio.

One thing month-to-month does not change: the 30-day notice in §5321.17 runs "prior to the periodic rental date," not from any random day. Give notice on the 10th and you are usually still paying for the following full month.

Negotiating: what is actually on the table (term length, repairs, pets, start date)

Rent is the least flexible item in a renewal. These four usually move:

  • Term length. An 18-month or 24-month renewal is often available at a lower monthly rate, because it removes two turns from the owner's calendar. Ask for it explicitly.
  • Repairs and upgrades. The single most effective renewal ask in this market: trade a signature for work that was already on the list. Put the scope and a completion date in the renewal itself, not in an email.
  • Start date. Shifting a renewal by 30 to 60 days can move your future move-out out of a Cleveland January and into a month when trucks, help and inventory are all cheaper.
  • Pets. Pet terms and pet deposits are negotiable — see renting with pets in Cleveland. Assistance animals are a separate matter entirely and are not pets: 24 CFR §100.204 makes it unlawful "to refuse to make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford a handicapped person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling unit."

Bring your ledger to the conversation. A clean 12-month payment history is the strongest argument any renter has, and it is a document you can download.

Renewal when you hold a CMHA voucher: the 60-day rent increase request and re-approval

On a voucher, renewal is a three-party transaction and the deadlines are federal.

  • No increase in the initial term. 24 CFR §982.309 requires the initial lease term to be at least one year, and states that "during the initial term of the lease, the owner may not raise the rent to owner."
  • Sixty days' notice to the PHA. 24 CFR §982.308(g)(4): "the owner must notify the PHA of any changes in the amount of the rent to owner at least sixty days before any such changes go into effect, and any such changes shall be subject to rent reasonableness requirements."
  • Rent reasonableness re-run. 24 CFR §982.507 requires the PHA to redetermine the reasonable rent "before any increase in the rent to owner," comparing location, quality, size, unit type, age, amenities, services, maintenance and utilities against comparable unassisted units.
  • Changing the term triggers a new HAP contract. §982.308(g)(2) provides that assistance shall not continue unless the PHA has approved a new tenancy and executed a new HAP contract where there are changes in lease provisions governing the term of the lease, or changes in who is responsible for utilities or appliances.

Two CMHA specifics worth holding onto. The agency sets its payment standards "between 90% and 110% of the FMRs or approved exceptions as applicable," and since January 1, 2025 it applies Small Area Fair Market Rent payment standards — meaning the standard varies by ZIP code within Cuyahoga County. And CMHA is explicit that "the payment standard is NOT the maximum amount that the landlord can charge; it is the maximum amount of subsidy that the CMHA will pay toward each tenant's rent portion." An approved increase can therefore raise your share even when the payment standard does not move. Recertification runs on its own calendar — see Section 8 recertification in Cleveland.

Deposit, renters insurance and utilities at renewal

Renewal is the moment to audit three things nobody looks at mid-lease.

Deposit interest. ORC §5321.16 provides that "any security deposit in excess of fifty dollars or one month's periodic rent, whichever is greater, shall bear interest on the excess at the rate of five per cent per annum if the tenant remains in possession of the premises for six months or more, and shall be computed and paid annually by the landlord to the tenant." If your deposit exceeds that threshold and you have been in place a year, the interest is an annual item — ask about it at renewal, when the anniversary is on everyone's desk. Our Ohio security deposit law guide covers the rest.

Renters insurance. Confirm the policy is still in force, that the address matches the unit exactly, and that the property manager is listed if the lease requires it. A lapsed policy discovered at renewal is a five-minute fix; discovered after a loss, it is not.

Utilities. Check which accounts are in your name and whether the renewal changes that split. On a voucher unit this is not cosmetic: §982.308(g)(2)(i) treats a change in "lease requirements governing tenant or owner responsibilities for utilities or appliances" as requiring a new tenancy approval and a new HAP contract, and the utility allowance feeds directly into what you pay in cash.

Deciding not to renew: notice, timing and the move-out chain

Give notice in writing, dated, and keep proof of delivery. If you are month-to-month, §5321.17 requires it "at least thirty days prior to the periodic rental date." If you are on a fixed term, the lease usually states its own window — commonly 30 to 60 days before the end date — and missing it is what converts a planned exit into a holdover.

Voucher holders have one extra step that is easy to miss: under §982.309(c)(1), if the family terminates the lease on notice to the owner, "the family must give the PHA a copy of the notice of termination at the same time," and failure to do so "is a breach of family obligations under the program." Same day, same notice, two recipients.

Then protect the deposit. Section 5321.16 gives the landlord 30 days "after termination of the rental agreement and delivery of possession" to return it, requires that "any deduction from the security deposit shall be itemized and identified by the landlord in a written notice," and requires that "the tenant shall provide the landlord in writing with a forwarding address or new address." Skip that forwarding address and you forfeit the right to damages and attorney's fees on a deposit wrongfully withheld — and where it is wrongfully withheld, the statute allows recovery of "damages in an amount equal to the amount wrongfully withheld, and reasonable attorneys fees."

If you need to leave before the term ends rather than at renewal, that is a different analysis — see breaking a lease in Ohio.

Frequently asked questions

How much notice does a landlord have to give before raising rent in Ohio?
Ohio's landlord-tenant chapter sets no separate notice period for a rent increase. What it sets is the notice to end a periodic tenancy: ORC §5321.17 provides that “the landlord or the tenant may terminate or fail to renew a month-to-month tenancy by notice given the other at least thirty days prior to the periodic rental date,” and seven days for a week-to-week tenancy. In practice that 30-day rule is what shapes month-to-month rent changes, because a landlord who wants a different rent has to end the current arrangement to offer a new one. On a fixed-term lease the rent cannot change mid-term unless the lease itself says so. Voucher units follow a separate 60-day federal rule.
What happens if my lease expires and I keep paying rent?
That depends on the holdover clause in your lease, not on a statute — ORC §5321.06 lets a landlord and tenant include “any term relating to rent, the duration of an agreement, and any other provisions governing the rights and obligations of the parties” that is not inconsistent with Chapter 5321, and holdover terms live there. Most Cleveland leases convert to a month-to-month tenancy, sometimes at a higher rate. Once you are month-to-month, ORC §5321.17's 30-day notice applies equally to you and to the landlord.
Is there rent control in Cleveland?
Ohio has no rent control statute, and since September 23, 2022, ORC §5321.20 preempts political subdivisions “from regulating the rights and obligations of parties to a rental agreement” under Chapter 5321. Renewal rent therefore comes from your lease and from the market. The nearest thing to an administrative ceiling applies only to voucher units, and it is not a cap on what a landlord may ask: under 24 CFR §982.507 the PHA must redetermine that the rent is reasonable compared with comparable unassisted units before any increase in the rent to owner.
Can my landlord raise the rent on a Section 8 unit?
Yes, but on a federal schedule. Under 24 CFR §982.309 the initial lease term must be at least one year and “during the initial term of the lease, the owner may not raise the rent to owner.” After that, 24 CFR §982.308(g)(4) requires that “the owner must notify the PHA of any changes in the amount of the rent to owner at least sixty days before any such changes go into effect, and any such changes shall be subject to rent reasonableness requirements.” 24 CFR §982.507 then requires the PHA to redetermine reasonable rent before any increase. CMHA also states that its payment standard “is NOT the maximum amount that the landlord can charge; it is the maximum amount of subsidy” it will pay.
How much notice do I have to give if I do not want to renew?
Read your lease first — a fixed-term lease usually states a notice window, often 30 to 60 days before the end date. If you are already month-to-month, ORC §5321.17 requires notice “at least thirty days prior to the periodic rental date,” and the same 30 days binds the landlord. If you hold a voucher, 24 CFR §982.309(c)(1) adds a step: if you give the owner notice of termination, “the family must give the PHA a copy of the notice of termination at the same time,” and failing to do so is a breach of family obligations under the program.
