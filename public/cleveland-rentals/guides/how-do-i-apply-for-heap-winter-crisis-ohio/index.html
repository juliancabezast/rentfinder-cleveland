How to Apply for HEAP and the Winter Crisis Program in Cuyahoga County

HEAP is a once-a-year credit applied straight to your gas, electric or bulk-fuel bill for Ohio households of eight or fewer at or below 175% of the federal poverty guidelines — $27,930 a year for one person, $57,750 for four, for July 2026 through May 2027. The Winter Crisis Program is the emergency version: it runs November 1 to March 31, pays up to $1,200 under the state’s draft plan for this program year, requires an appointment with your local provider, and covers not just shutoffs but the cost of transferring service or establishing new service when you move. Call (800) 282-0880 to be routed to your local provider.

Updated August 8, 2026 ·8 min read ·By the Rent Finder Cleveland team

What HEAP is, and how the Winter Crisis Program differs from regular HEAP

The Ohio Department of Job and Family Services describes HEAP plainly: “The Home Energy Assistance Program (HEAP) is a federally funded program that provides eligible Ohioans assistance with their home energy bills. This one-time benefit is applied directly to the customer’s utility bill or bulk fuel bill.” You do not receive a check. The credit lands on the account.

Two things about that sentence matter for renters in Cleveland. First, one-time means once per program year — regular HEAP is not a monthly subsidy, and if you are looking for something that lowers every bill instead of one bill, you want PIPP Plus, not HEAP. Second, bulk fuel bill is in there because a meaningful share of older Ohio housing still heats with delivered fuel oil or propane rather than a metered line.

The Winter Crisis Program is a separate component of HEAP, administered — per the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio — “by community action agencies throughout Ohio,” with “heating assistance for income eligible Ohioans… available November 1 through March 31.” Regular HEAP is a benefit you apply for and wait on. Winter Crisis is a benefit you apply for because something is already going wrong, and it moves on an appointment calendar instead of a 12-week queue.

One administrative note that trips up anyone following an older bookmark: as of April 6, 2026 these programs moved to ODJFS. The state’s own wording is “Effective April 6, 2026, this program will be administered by the Ohio Department of Job and Family Services. There will be no interruption of service or change in the application process.” The old energyhelp.ohio.gov address now redirects to jfs.ohio.gov rather than serving its own content. That matters because fact sheets still in circulation (including the Ohio Consumers’ Counsel one cited below) send readers to the old domain. The live page is the HEAP page on jfs.ohio.gov.

Who qualifies: the 175% of federal poverty guideline test

ODJFS states the rule as “Ohioans with a household size of eight or less, and income at or below 175 percent of the federal poverty guidelines.” These are the limits it publishes for the July 2026 – May 2027 period, in both of the forms the state accepts, copied from the ODJFS table:

The two columns are the reason a lot of people who assume they are over the limit are not. A household can qualify on 30-day income or on 12-month income. If you were laid off in June, your last 30 days may clear the test even though your annual figure does not. ODJFS prefers 12 months but accepts 30 days.

Larger households are measured against 60% of the State Median Income rather than 175% FPG. Read the state’s page carefully here, because its sentence and its table do not line up: ODJFS introduces the figures as being “for households with nine or more members,” but the table that follows starts at eight. Above twelve people the state does not publish a figure. Do not extrapolate it yourself — have the provider run the number when you call.

What counts as income: “total gross household income for the past 30 days (12 months preferred) for all members, except wage or salary income earned by dependent minors under 18 years old.” A working 17-year-old’s paycheck does not push your household over the line. And the state is explicit that “Both homeowners and renters are eligible for assistance” — renting is not a disqualifier.

One number to ignore. PUCO’s Winter Crisis page currently prints $29,930 as the one-person limit for 2026-2027. Every other state table — HEAP, Summer Crisis, the same 175% FPG basis — prints $27,930, and both tables step up by exactly $9,940 per additional member from $27,930 to $37,870. The $29,930 is a typo. Apply against $27,930.

The crisis window: November 1 through March 31, and why you should not wait

One caveat before the dates and dollar figures below: for this program year ODJFS has published only a draft HEAP State Plan (the file is named 2026-2027_Draft_HEAP_State_Plan.pdf), where the two prior years are posted as final. Benefit maximums are exactly the sort of figure that can move between a draft and an approved plan, so confirm any dollar amount with your provider before you count on it. With that said, the draft plan lists the components on two different clocks: “Heating assistance 10/01/2026 04/30/2027… Winter Crisis assistance 11/01/2026 03/31/2027.” Regular HEAP opens a month earlier and closes a month later than the crisis program.

Waiting is expensive for a specific, mechanical reason. Regular HEAP and PIPP applications carry a stated processing time — ODJFS says “There is a 12-week processing time for both online and mailed applications,” and the same ODJFS HEAP page asks applicants to allow 90 days for a regular HEAP or PIPP application to process. Twelve weeks from a November 1 filing is late January. If you file regular HEAP in October, the credit can land before the coldest stretch. If you file in January, it may not.

Crisis does not follow that queue, but it has its own bottleneck: appointments. The draft State Plan is direct — “While an appointment is required for crisis assistance, the client can enter in their information and upload documentation online to expedite the intake process once at the local agency.” ODJFS makes the same point on its HEAP page: applying online for the Crisis Program does not schedule you — you still have to contact your local community action agency to set the appointment. Filing online does not book the slot. Calling books the slot.

What Winter Crisis covers is broader than most renters realize. Eligible situations include households “facing disconnection, have been disconnected, need to establish new services, need to pay to transfer service, have PIPP default, need to pay first PIPP, or have less than a 25 percent supply of bulk fuel in their tank.” Read that list again with a move in mind: establishing new service and paying to transfer service are both covered. If you are moving into a Cleveland rental between November and March and the deposit to turn on gas is the thing standing in your way, that is a Winter Crisis conversation, not just a shutoff conversation. Our guide to setting up utilities as a Cleveland renter walks through the account side of that.

Where to apply in Cuyahoga County and how to get an appointment

Here is where we have to be honest rather than helpful-sounding. Ohio runs energy assistance through local providers — the draft State Plan says “All applications can be completed at the 51 local Energy Assistance Provider locations around the 88 counties” — but as of this writing the state does not publish a working page naming the Cuyahoga County provider with an address, phone number and office hours. The provider detail page we checked on jfs.ohio.gov returns a 404, and the provider map is an ArcGIS application that does not render as text.

So do not chase an agency name off a blog. Use the number the state itself publishes for exactly this problem: “To be directed to your local provider: (800) 282-0880.” That line exists because the directory is unreliable.

Your three channels, per ODJFS: online with an OHID account through the Energy Assistance Portal, in person or by phone with the local provider, or by mail. Two of the three are slow (12 weeks). A crisis is resolved on the appointment track with your local provider instead of in that queue.

If a disability makes the trip to the office impossible, say so when you call. The same draft State Plan that requires an appointment for crisis assistance also commits the state to giving people with a disability a way to submit a crisis application without leaving home. The appointment requirement is real, but it is not a reason to skip applying — ask the provider for that accommodation directly.

One more practical trap: the online application does not work on a phone. ODJFS states it outright — “To apply for Energy Assistance Programs online, you must use a PC via Chrome, Firefox, Safari, or Microsoft Edge. The application process is not currently accessible using Internet Explorer or a mobile device such as a smartphone or tablet.” If your only device is a phone, plan on a library computer or the in-person route from the start.

Documents to bring for every household member

ODJFS lists four categories, and the words “each” and “all” are doing real work — a missing document for one household member stalls the whole file.

  • Proof of income for each household member — the state names “Supplemental Security Income (SSI), Social Security Disability Income (SSDI), Social Security, Pension, Alimony, Wages, Self-employment, Unemployment, etc.”
  • Copies of your most recent utility bills — the actual bills, not a screenshot of a balance.
  • A list of all household members, including Social Security numbers and birth dates.
  • Proof of U.S. citizenship or legal residency for all household members — accepted documents are “a Social Security Card, birth certificate, U.S. Passport, naturalization paper/certification of citizenship, permanent VISA, or INS ID Card.”

If you are heading to a crisis appointment, upload these through the portal first. The draft State Plan says that is precisely what shortens intake once you are in the office.

What the benefit actually pays and how often you can get it

Regular HEAP heating assistance is smaller than most people expect. The draft 2026-2027 State Plan sets a “Minimum Benefit $31 Maximum Benefit $546.00.” The crisis components are much larger: “Winter Crisis Maximum Benefit $1,200 Summer Crisis Maximum Benefit $2,300.” All four are draft figures until the approved plan is posted.

Your specific amount depends on income and poverty percentage, fuel source, location within the state, and PIPP enrollment. Two of those deserve a flag. First, the plan says “A weighted benefit is given to clients who have someone in the home over the age of 60” — households with an older adult receive more. Second, and this catches people: “those on both HEAP and PIPP receive a 75% HEAP reduction.” Being on PIPP is usually the better deal overall, but it shrinks your HEAP credit to a quarter of what it would otherwise be. Do not budget for both at full value.

The exact benefit matrix by fuel type and region is referenced in the draft State Plan as an attachment that is not published in the PDF itself, so we cannot tell you what a Cleveland household on natural gas receives. Only the $31 floor and $546 ceiling are on the record.

Summer Crisis Program and cooling assistance

ODJFS: “The Home Energy Assistance Summer Crisis Program provides a one-time benefit to eligible Ohioans with cooling assistance during the summer months. The program runs from July 1 to Sept. 30.” PUCO notes program dates may vary year to year, and the draft 2026-2027 State Plan logs the component on a different span, so confirm the current window when you call.

Summer Crisis adds a condition on top of the 175% income test. The household must have “a household member who is either: 60 years of age and older” or “can provide physician documentation that cooling assistance is needed for a household member’s health.” Separately, “households who have a disconnect notice, have been shut off, or are trying to establish new electric service may be eligible.”

Equipment rules are strict and worth knowing before you ask: one air conditioner per household, “provided the household has not received an air conditioner in the prior program year,” and “no more than two fans, once every other program year.”

Applying while you rent: what your landlord does and does not need to sign

If the gas and electric accounts are in your name, your landlord is not part of this application at all. No signature, no letter, no permission.

If the bill is in the landlord’s name, the draft State Plan sets one requirement: “Renters whose bill is in the landlord’s name must produce a lease or written documentation from the landlord verifying that the renter is responsible for the electric bill, and/or the gas bill.” A lease clause that assigns utilities to you is usually enough on its own — check your lease before you ask for a letter.

Subsidized housing is the one place the rule bites. “Tenants whose utility bills are not in the renter’s name(s) are ineligible to receive benefits, unless they provide verification that they pay all or a portion (i.e., HUD Section 8 housing) of the utility bill.” Voucher holders who pay part of a utility bill are not shut out — they just have to document the portion. If you hold a Housing Choice Voucher, the interaction with your utility allowance is worth understanding before you file, and our overview of which utilities are typically included in Cleveland rent covers who is on the hook for what in a standard local lease.

If you are denied: appeal steps and what to try next

ODJFS does not publish a HEAP-specific appeal procedure on the program pages cited here, so we are not going to invent one. Call your provider and the state line at (800) 282-0880 and ask for the denial reason in writing — most denials we hear about are documentation gaps, not income failures, and a documentation gap is fixable in the same program year.

In the meantime, several protections apply regardless of whether HEAP approves you, as long as your utility is regulated by PUCO:

  • Extended payment plans. PUCO describes three, and they are open to everyone: the 1/3 plan (Winter Heating Season Plan, offered only November 1 to April 15), the 1/6 plan, and the 1/9 plan. PUCO’s language is unambiguous — “These plans are available to all residential customers regardless of income.”
  • The Special Reconnect Order. “Any residential customer… regardless of income, may pay $175 to use the Special Reconnect Order to establish, maintain, or restore service,” once per winter heating season. It can also be used to start new service by paying $175 instead of a full security deposit. The 2025-2026 order ran October 13 to April 15 with a reconnection fee of no more than $36 up front; PUCO issues the order annually, typically in the fall, so confirm this season’s dates.
  • The 30-day medical certificate. It postpones disconnection for nonpayment and “may be used no more than 3 times in a rolling 12-month period,” completed by a licensed physician, physician assistant, clinical nurse specialist, certified nurse practitioner, certified nurse-midwife, or local board of health physician.
  • Third-party notice. You can name someone — an adult child, a caseworker — to receive disconnection notices, and the company must notify them at least fourteen days before shutting service off. Receiving the notice does not make them liable for the bill.
  • Fourteen days’ notice. A regulated company may disconnect for nonpayment only “after at least fourteen days’ notice… during normal utility company business hours.”

The Cleveland-specific catch: Cleveland Public Power is a division of the City of Cleveland, and Ohio Revised Code §4905.02(A)(3) excludes “a public utility that is owned or operated by any municipal corporation” from PUCO’s definition of a public utility. None of the PUCO protections above — payment plans, the Special Reconnect Order, PIPP — apply to a CPP account. For CPP you call Customer Care at (216) 664-4600. Which company serves your address decides which rulebook you get, and our CPP vs. The Illuminating Company comparison is the place to start. PUCO’s general assistance line is (800) 686-7826.

Frequently asked questions

What is the income limit for HEAP in Ohio?
HEAP is open to households of eight or fewer with income at or below 175% of the federal poverty guidelines. For July 2026 through May 2027, ODJFS publishes 12-month limits of $27,930 for one person, $37,870 for two, $47,810 for three, $57,750 for four, $67,690 for five, $77,630 for six and $87,570 for seven. You may qualify on either 30-day income or 12-month income — the matching 30-day limits run from $2,295.62 for one person to $7,197.53 for seven. Larger households are measured against 60% of the State Median Income instead, on a second ODJFS table that runs from $97,546 for eight to $106,029 for twelve.
When does the Winter Crisis Program open and close?
Ohio’s draft 2026-2027 HEAP State Plan — the only version ODJFS has posted for this program year — lists Winter Crisis assistance running November 1, 2026 through March 31, 2027. The regular HEAP heating component runs longer, October 1, 2026 through April 30, 2027. One PUCO page still says the crisis program runs through May 1; the state plan and PUCO’s own dedicated crisis page both say March 31, so plan around March 31.
Where do I apply for HEAP in Cuyahoga County?
Ohio routes applicants through local Energy Assistance Providers — 51 offices covering all 88 counties — and does not publish a working text directory naming the Cuyahoga agency with its phone and hours. Call the state line at (800) 282-0880 to be directed to your local provider. You can also apply online through the Energy Assistance Portal or by mail, but both carry a 12-week processing time.
Can I get HEAP if the utility bill is not in my name?
Yes, if you can prove you pay it. Ohio’s draft HEAP State Plan says renters whose bill is in the landlord’s name must produce a lease or written documentation from the landlord verifying that the renter is responsible for the electric and/or gas bill. Tenants in subsidized housing whose bills are not in their name are ineligible unless they verify they pay all or a portion of the utility bill.
How much does the Winter Crisis Program pay?
Ohio’s draft 2026-2027 HEAP State Plan sets a Winter Crisis maximum benefit of $1,200 per household and a Summer Crisis maximum of $2,300. Regular HEAP heating assistance is much smaller: a $31 minimum and a $546 maximum. Those are the draft plan’s figures; ODJFS has not posted an approved plan for this program year, so confirm the amount with your provider. The exact amount depends on your income, fuel source, location in the state and whether you are enrolled in PIPP.
