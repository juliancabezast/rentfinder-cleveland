Your Rental Application Was Denied in Ohio: Adverse Action and What to Do Next

If a landlord denied you based even partly on a consumer report, federal law owes you a specific thing: an adverse action notice naming the screening company and its address — plus a toll-free number, but only where that agency keeps files on consumers nationwide — a statement that the company did not make the decision, and notice of your right to a free copy of that report within 60 days and to dispute it. Get the report, find the exact item that caused the denial, dispute it in writing if it is wrong (the agency generally has 30 days, extendable to 45), and fix or offset it if it is right. If the reason looks like a protected characteristic, you have one year to file with HUD and one year to file with the Ohio Civil Rights Commission.

Updated August 8, 2026 ·8 min read ·By the Rent Finder Cleveland team

The adverse action notice: what the landlord must send you and when

The moment a landlord uses a consumer report to say no — or to say yes but on worse terms, like a larger deposit — the Fair Credit Reporting Act calls that an adverse action, and it triggers a notice obligation on the landlord, not on the screening company.

15 U.S.C. §1681m requires the user of the report to provide "oral, written, or electronic notice of the adverse action to the consumer." The notice has to identify the consumer reporting agency by name, address and telephone number — the statute adds a toll-free telephone number only for agencies that compile and maintain files on consumers on a nationwide basis, which many tenant-screening companies are not — and it has to say plainly that "the consumer reporting agency did not make the decision to take the adverse action." It must also tell you about two rights: a free copy of the report within 60 days, and the right to dispute the accuracy of the information under §1681i.

Two practical notes for Cleveland applicants. First, "oral" counts — a phone call from a leasing agent can satisfy the statute, so write down the date, the name of the person, and the screening company they named. Second, the notice tells you where the file lives, which is the single most useful thing on it. Most tenant-screening companies are not the big three bureaus; the file that sank your application may be one you have never pulled.

If the denial had nothing to do with a report — the unit was leased to someone who applied an hour earlier — no adverse action notice is required. Ask which it was.

Getting the report free: your 60-day right under the FCRA

Call the screening company named in the notice, not the landlord. 15 U.S.C. §1681m requires the adverse action notice to tell you two things on this point: that you may obtain a free copy of your consumer report from the agency that supplied it, within the 60-day window §1681j sets for that free copy, and that you may dispute with that agency the accuracy or completeness of anything in the file under §1681i (official text). Start that clock the day of the denial, because 60 days is shorter than it sounds once you are also apartment hunting.

Do it in parallel with the other free file you are entitled to. 15 U.S.C. §1681j requires the nationwide agencies to make your §1681g disclosures "once during any 12-month period upon request of the consumer and without charge to the consumer," through the centralized source. Pulling both lets you compare: if the item that caused the denial appears on the screening report but not on the bureau file, you are looking at a court-record match, and that is a different kind of dispute.

Reading the denial reason: credit, income, eviction record, or something else

Sort the reason into one of four buckets, because the fix is different for each.

  • Credit. Usually a collection account or a balance owed to a prior landlord rather than a score. Check the age: under 15 U.S.C. §1681c, a collection account and "any other adverse item of information" cannot be reported past seven years, and a bankruptcy cannot be reported past 10.
  • Income. Almost always an arithmetic gap against the landlord's own multiple. Ohio sets no income-to-rent ratio by statute — but whatever ratio a landlord uses has to be applied identically to everyone under 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4), which bars "different qualification criteria or applications … such as income standards, application requirements, application fees, credit analysis" because of a protected characteristic.
  • Eviction record. Check the disposition. A filing that was dismissed still creates a docket entry, and a report that shows the filing without the outcome is inaccurate, not merely unflattering. Under §1681c, civil suits and judgments run out at seven years "or until the governing statute of limitations has expired, whichever is the longer period."
  • Criminal record. §1681c's seven-year limit covers arrest records but expressly excludes "records of convictions of crimes," so a conviction has no federal reporting age limit. Be aware that HUD's April 4, 2016 OGC guidance on criminal records was listed as no longer expressing the Office of General Counsel's official legal opinion in a HUD General Counsel memorandum on fair housing guidance memoranda and documents — so older advice that leans on that document is not current.

Disputing an error with the screening company, step by step

Disputes go to the consumer reporting agency, in writing, with documents attached. 15 U.S.C. §1681i requires the agency to "conduct a reasonable reinvestigation to determine whether the disputed information is inaccurate … or delete the item from the file … before the end of the 30-day period beginning on the date on which the agency receives the notice of the dispute."

That 30-day window "may be extended for not more than 15 additional days if the consumer reporting agency receives information from the consumer during that 30-day period that is relevant to the reinvestigation." In other words, sending more paperwork mid-dispute can buy the agency two more weeks — worth knowing if you are trying to clear a file before a specific move-in date. Send everything at once if speed matters.

What to attach, in order of usefulness: the certified court docket showing dismissal or satisfaction; a paid-in-full or zero-balance letter from the original creditor; the itemized deposit statement your prior landlord was required to give you under ORC §5321.16 ("any deduction from the security deposit shall be itemized and identified by the landlord in a written notice"); and proof of identity if the item belongs to someone else with a similar name.

Keep the dispute confirmation. When you reapply, handing the next landlord a dated dispute receipt plus the underlying court document is far more persuasive than an explanation.

When the denial may be discriminatory: protected classes and source of income in Cleveland

Ohio's list is specific. ORC §4112.02(H) makes it unlawful to "refuse to sell, transfer, assign, rent, lease, sublease, or finance housing accommodations … because of race, color, religion, sex, military status, familial status, ancestry, disability, or national origin." Federal regulation adds the operational rule at 24 CFR §100.60(b)(4): different criteria, different application requirements, different application fees or different credit analysis by protected class are unlawful. And 24 CFR §100.204 makes it unlawful "to refuse to make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford a handicapped person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling unit" — the provision behind assistance-animal requests at a no-pets property.

Source of income is the part where honesty matters more than a comforting answer. It is not on the Ohio state list above, so under state law a voucher is not a protected characteristic. Whether the particular municipality you applied in has passed its own ordinance is a separate question, and we keep that answer on one page rather than two: source of income discrimination in Cleveland, Ohio explains why the City of Cleveland itself does not protect voucher holders, and names the handful of suburbs that do. Note also that HUD's own guidance document "Application of Fair Housing and Civil Rights Laws to Source of Income Restrictions" (November 4, 2024) is on the list of documents the HUD General Counsel stated no longer express the office's official legal opinion.

If you are renting in Milwaukee, the statute reads differently. Wisconsin's state fair housing law (Wis. Stat. §106.50(1m)(nm)) does list lawful source of income among its protected classes, alongside race, color, sex, religion, national origin, disability, marital status and family status, among others. The catch is that the statute never defines the term, and that gap is where the two wrinkles worth knowing come from. On one side, the Equal Rights Division's rule (Wis. Admin. Code §DWD 220.02(8)) defines lawful source of income deliberately openly — it includes, but is not limited to, any negotiable draft, coupon or voucher representing monetary value — but its only example is food stamps, and it never names Housing Choice Vouchers. On the other side, the official text of the statute carries an annotation pointing to Knapp v. Eagle Property Management Corp., 54 F.3d 1272 (1995), which it summarizes as holding that federal rent vouchers are not clearly within the meaning of the term. Both are still published today, and the state itself sets them side by side in its own decision digest without harmonizing them.

So we are not going to tell you that a private landlord in Wisconsin may turn down a Housing Choice Voucher, and we are not going to tell you that one may not. The courts have not settled that tension, and anyone who states it categorically — in either direction — is saying more than the sources say. If you were denied in Milwaukee and you believe your voucher is the reason, talk to a housing attorney licensed in Wisconsin: the outcome turns on the specific facts and on whatever local rules apply. The one thing we can tell you without any qualification is our own position, and it is this: Rent Finder Cleveland accepts Housing Choice Vouchers in every home we manage, in Milwaukee and in every market where we operate. We do not need to wait for a court to resolve it in order to do that.

Where to file: Ohio Civil Rights Commission, HUD Form 903, Legal Aid

You can pursue both tracks, and both have a one-year clock.

  • HUD. 42 U.S.C. §3610: "An aggrieved person may, not later than one year after an alleged discriminatory housing practice has occurred or terminated, file a complaint with the Secretary." HUD takes the complaint on its Report Housing Discrimination page, online or by printing form HUD-903.1 and mailing it to the regional FHEO office. The toll-free line is 1-800-669-9777.
  • Ohio Civil Rights Commission. ORC §4112.05 sets six months for most charges, but for housing under division (H) of §4112.02 the charge "shall be in writing and under oath and shall be filed with the commission within one year after the alleged unlawful discriminatory practice was committed."
  • Legal aid. The Legal Aid Society of Cleveland provides free civil legal help in Cuyahoga and neighboring counties, and is a sensible first call for a tenant who needs someone to read the file, not just a form to fill out. Bring the adverse action notice, the screening report, and your dated application receipt.

File on time even if you are unsure. The clock in both statutes runs from when the practice "occurred or terminated," not from when you figured out what happened.

Reapplying stronger: co-signer, larger deposit, second-chance listings

Most denials on a moderately priced Cleveland rental come down to one line item, and there are three standard offsets.

  • A co-signer. Adds verified income to the file. See our guide to using a co-signer to rent an apartment in Ohio for what the co-signer is actually agreeing to.
  • A larger deposit. Ohio does not cap the deposit amount by statute, so this is negotiable — but know the trade. Under ORC §5321.16, "any security deposit in excess of fifty dollars or one month's periodic rent, whichever is greater, shall bear interest on the excess at the rate of five per cent per annum if the tenant remains in possession of the premises for six months or more," and it is "computed and paid annually by the landlord to the tenant." A bigger deposit is money the landlord owes interest on and owes back, itemized, within 30 days of termination and delivery of possession. Details in our Ohio security deposit law guide.
  • Owners who publish criteria. Start with second-chance houses for rent in Cleveland and our guides on renting with bad credit and renting with an eviction on your record.

One more thing that costs nothing: give the forwarding address in writing when you leave your current place. ORC §5321.16 provides that "the tenant shall provide the landlord in writing with a forwarding address or new address" — skip it and you give up the right to damages and attorney's fees on a wrongfully withheld deposit, which is exactly the money you need for the next application.

How to keep a denial from costing you application fees again

Ohio publishes no statutory cap on rental application fees, and no statutory refund right. That makes the fee a pure sunk cost every time you apply blind. Three habits fix it:

  • Ask for the written criteria before you pay. Minimum income, how collections are treated, how old an eviction filing must be. An owner that cannot produce it is an owner who cannot show consistency either.
  • Pull your own file first using the §1681j free annual disclosure, and clear the obvious errors before anyone else reads it.
  • Apply with the paperwork already assembled — pay stubs or benefit award letters, bank statements, prior landlord ledger, dispute receipts. Our Ohio rental application fee guide covers what is normal in this market.

Frequently asked questions

Does a landlord have to tell me why my rental application was denied?
If the decision was based even in part on a consumer report, yes — 15 U.S.C. §1681m requires the user of the report to give you “oral, written, or electronic notice of the adverse action to the consumer,” identify the consumer reporting agency by name, address and telephone number — a toll-free number is required only of agencies that compile files on consumers nationwide — and make clear that “the consumer reporting agency did not make the decision to take the adverse action.” The statute requires the notice and the source; it does not require a detailed narrative of the landlord's reasoning.
How do I get a free copy of the tenant screening report used against me?
Two separate rights apply. After an adverse action, 15 U.S.C. §1681m gives you the right to obtain a free copy of the report within 60 days and to dispute the accuracy of the information under §1681i. Independently, 15 U.S.C. §1681j requires the nationwide agencies to give you your file disclosure “once during any 12-month period upon request of the consumer and without charge to the consumer” through the centralized source. Ask the screening company named in the adverse action notice — that is the agency holding the file the landlord actually read.
How long do I have to file a housing discrimination complaint in Ohio?
One year, on both tracks. Federally, 42 U.S.C. §3610 says an aggrieved person may file with HUD “not later than one year after an alleged discriminatory housing practice has occurred or terminated.” Under Ohio law, ORC §4112.05 sets a six-month deadline for most charges but provides that for housing discrimination under division (H) of ORC §4112.02, “the charge shall be in writing and under oath and shall be filed with the commission within one year after the alleged unlawful discriminatory practice was committed.”
Can I be denied for having a Housing Choice Voucher in Cleveland?
Ohio's statutory list of protected classes in housing, at ORC §4112.02(H), is race, color, religion, sex, military status, familial status, ancestry, disability and national origin — source of income is not on that list, so at the state level a voucher is not a protected characteristic. Whether the particular municipality has passed its own ordinance is a separate question, and our page on source of income discrimination in Cleveland, Ohio explains why the City of Cleveland itself does not protect voucher holders and names the handful of suburbs that do. Our own position is not ambiguous: we accept Housing Choice Vouchers in every home we manage, by our own choice rather than because any law makes us.
Do I get my application fee back if I am denied?
Not automatically. We found no section of the Ohio Revised Code and no City of Cleveland ordinance that caps rental application fees or requires a refund after a denial — the amount and the refund policy come from the landlord's own terms, so get them in writing before you pay. For contrast, Wisconsin does regulate one piece of this: Wis. Stat. §704.085 limits a landlord to its actual cost, up to $25, for a consumer credit report, and bars the charge entirely if you supply your own nationwide-bureau report less than 30 days old. Our Ohio rental application fee guide is the fuller treatment.
