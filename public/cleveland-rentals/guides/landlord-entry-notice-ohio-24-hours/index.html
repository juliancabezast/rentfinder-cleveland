How Much Notice Must a Landlord Give Before Entering in Ohio?

Ohio law requires a landlord to give reasonable notice of intent to enter and to enter only at reasonable times, and ORC 5321.04(A)(8) states that "Twenty-four hours is presumed to be a reasonable notice in the absence of evidence to the contrary." The statute carves out two exceptions in its opening words — emergency, and where notice is impracticable. If a landlord enters in violation of that rule, enters lawfully but in an unreasonable manner, or makes repeated entry demands that harass you, ORC 5321.04(B) lets you recover actual damages, get an injunction, get reasonable attorney's fees, or terminate the lease.

What ORC 5321.04(A)(8) actually says about entry

Most of what renters hear about entry in Ohio is a summary of a summary. Here is the operative sentence, word for word, from section 5321.04 of the Revised Code, in the list of things a landlord shall do:

"Except in the case of emergency or if it is impracticable to do so, give the tenant reasonable notice of the landlord's intent to enter and enter only at reasonable times. Twenty-four hours is presumed to be a reasonable notice in the absence of evidence to the contrary."

Read it carefully, because three separate rules are packed into two sentences. First, notice must be reasonable — 24 hours is not a hard statutory minimum, it is a legal presumption that 24 hours clears the bar. Second, the entry itself must be at a reasonable time, which is a distinct requirement: perfect notice for a 6:00 a.m. visit still fails the second half. Third, the whole obligation is switched off by emergency or impracticability.

"Presumed … in the absence of evidence to the contrary" cuts both ways, and that is the part almost nobody explains. It means a landlord who gave you 24 hours starts out on the right side of the line, but you can still show that in your circumstances it was not enough. It also means that less than 24 hours is not automatically illegal — a landlord could argue that four hours was reasonable for a plumber whose availability appeared that morning. In practice the presumption is why 24 hours has become the operating standard for every competent Cleveland property manager: it is the one number you never have to argue about.

One more provision sits alongside it. ORC 5321.04(A)(7) separately requires a landlord to "Not abuse the right of access conferred by division (B) of section 5321.05" — that is, the right to come in for repairs and showings is a right that can be abused, and abusing it is its own violation.

What counts as 24 hours notice: text, email, note on the door

The statute says "reasonable notice." It does not say written notice. It does not require certified mail, a signed acknowledgment, or any particular format, and it does not mention text messages or email — the section predates all of it. So the honest answer to "does a text count?" is that Ohio law does not prescribe a channel, and the question is only whether the notice was reasonable in the circumstances.

What actually decides that is content and timing, not medium. Notice that names a date, a time window, and a reason is very hard to attack. "Someone may stop by this week" is not notice of an intent to enter on a given day; it is a warning that entry might happen sometime, which leaves you no ability to plan around it.

  • Text message — no legal problem, and it timestamps itself. Do not delete the thread.
  • Email — same, with the advantage that it survives a lost phone.
  • Note taped to the door — common in Cleveland two-family and four-suite buildings. It works, but photograph it, because a paper note is the one form of notice with no automatic record of when it was posted.
  • Phone call or voicemail — a call is notice if it conveys the intent to enter. Send a short confirming text afterward ("confirming Thursday 1–3 p.m. for the water heater") so both of you have the same record.
  • A clause in the lease — a lease term saying you consent in advance to entry at any time does not repeal the statute. Note also that ORC 5321.13(D) says no agreement by a tenant limiting the landlord's liability arising under law, or indemnifying the landlord for it, is recognized.

If you rent a unit where entry has been a problem, the single most useful habit is to answer every entry notice in writing, even when you agree: "Received — Thursday 1–3 p.m. works." It costs you nothing, and it converts a stream of one-way notices into a two-way record with dates on it.

Reasonable times: what hours a landlord may enter

Ohio does not publish a clock. There is no statutory "8 a.m. to 8 p.m." window in Chapter 5321 — the legislature wrote "reasonable times" and left it to be applied case by case. That vagueness is not an accident: what is reasonable for a vacant duplex mid-renovation is not reasonable for a household on second shift.

Because there is no fixed window, the facts that matter are the ones you can document. Normal business hours on a weekday are the safest ground for a landlord. Entry before dawn, late at night, or repeatedly on a day you have said you cannot be home invites the argument that the entry was made in an unreasonable manner — which, as the next sections show, is its own separate violation under 5321.04(B) even when the notice itself was fine.

If your schedule genuinely makes standard hours impossible, say so once, in writing, and propose two alternatives. A tenant who has offered workable windows is in a very different position than one who simply says no, because of the obligation described further down.

The emergency exception, and what is not an emergency

The exception is real and it is broad in the moment: "Except in the case of emergency or if it is impracticable to do so." A burst supply line flooding the unit below, gas odor, smoke, a fire alarm sounding in an empty unit — nobody is required to wait 24 hours for any of that, and no reasonable tenant would want them to.

What the exception does not do is convert routine work into an emergency because it is inconvenient to schedule. A landlord's own delay does not manufacture urgency. Neither does an appraisal, a photographer, a leasing showing, a lender's inspection, or a contractor's bid. Those are ordinary entries under 5321.05(B) and they require ordinary notice.

"Impracticable" is the sleeper clause. It covers the case where notice cannot realistically be given at all — nobody reachable, no working phone number, a unit the tenant has clearly abandoned. It is not a general convenience escape hatch, and a landlord who has your phone number and uses it every month for rent will have a hard time claiming notice was impracticable.

Entry for showings when you are still living there

This is the most common Cleveland friction point, because the market here moves on 30-day notice cycles: your landlord wants to re-lease the unit before you have finished moving out, and starts booking showings on top of your packed boxes.

They are allowed to. ORC 5321.05(B) says the tenant "shall not unreasonably withhold consent for the landlord to enter into the dwelling unit in order to inspect the premises, make ordinary, necessary, or agreed repairs, decorations, alterations, or improvements, deliver parcels that are too large for the tenant's mail facilities, supply necessary or agreed services, or exhibit the dwelling unit to prospective or actual purchasers, mortgagees, tenants, workmen, or contractors." Showings to prospective tenants are on that list by name.

What you can insist on is that showings run through the same door as everything else: notice, reasonable hour, stated purpose. A landlord who wants to show the unit six times a week during your last month is stacking entries, and stacked entries are exactly what the harassment language in 5321.04(B) is aimed at. If you are in your final 30 days, our guide to month-to-month tenancies in Ohio explains how the notice period itself works.

Your side of the deal: not unreasonably withholding consent under ORC 5321.05

Chapter 5321 is symmetrical, and the symmetry has teeth. Blanket refusal to allow entry is a violation of 5321.05(B) by the tenant, and ORC 5321.05(C)(1) gives the landlord a remedy for it: if the tenant violates the section, the landlord "may recover any actual damages that result from the violation together with reasonable attorney's fees," in addition to any right to terminate the rental agreement, sue for possession, or obtain injunctive relief to compel access.

That is why the strategy of changing the locks or refusing all access to force a repair is a bad one. It converts you from the party with the statutory claim into the party with the statutory exposure. The correct move when a landlord will not fix something is the written-notice route in ORC 5321.07 — not blocking the door.

Repeated demands and harassment: what the statute calls abuse of access

ORC 5321.04(B) names three distinct wrongs, and it is worth separating them because tenants usually only know about the first:

  • Entry in violation of (A)(8) — no notice, or entry at an unreasonable time.
  • A lawful entry made in an unreasonable manner — the notice was fine and the hour was fine, but the conduct was not.
  • Repeated demands for entry, otherwise lawful, that have the effect of harassing the tenant — each individual demand is legal; the pattern is the violation.

That third category is the one that fits the real complaint most renters have. Note the phrase "that have the effect of harassing" — the statute is written around effect, which is why a calendar matters more than an accusation. Keep a plain log: date, time, who, stated reason, whether notice was given and when. Six lines in a notes app, kept contemporaneously, is more persuasive than a paragraph written from memory months later.

Your remedies: written demand, injunction, damages, attorney fees

Here is the remedy sentence in full, from 5321.04(B): if the landlord "makes an entry in violation of division (A)(8) of this section, makes a lawful entry in an unreasonable manner, or makes repeated demands for entry otherwise lawful that have the effect of harassing the tenant, the tenant may recover actual damages resulting from the entry or demands, obtain injunctive relief to prevent the recurrence of the conduct, and obtain a judgment for reasonable attorney's fees, or may terminate the rental agreement."

Four things follow from that text.

  • Damages are actual damages. There is no statutory penalty figure for entry violations the way there is for wrongfully withheld deposits under 5321.16(C). You recover what you can prove you lost.
  • Injunctive relief is available. When the real problem is that it keeps happening, an order to stop is often worth more than money.
  • Attorney's fees are recoverable — which is what makes these cases economically possible at all. Note the contrast with ORC 5321.13(C), which says no agreement to pay the landlord's or tenant's attorney's fees is recognized in a residential rental agreement. Fees here come from the statute, not from a lease clause.
  • Termination is an option, not a requirement — the sentence ends "or may terminate the rental agreement."

ORC 5321.12 backs this up generally: in any action under Chapter 5321, "any party may recover damages for the breach of contract or the breach of any duty that is imposed by law." Before any of that, send one dated written demand describing the entries and asking for the practice to stop. It is not a statutory prerequisite for an entry claim the way written notice is for repairs — but it is the document that turns "he keeps coming in" into a documented request the landlord ignored.

Retaliation after you complain: what ORC 5321.02 forbids

The fear that stops most renters from saying anything is that a complaint will end the tenancy. Ohio addresses that directly. Under ORC 5321.02(A), a landlord may not retaliate "by increasing the tenant's rent, decreasing services that are due to the tenant, or bringing or threatening to bring an action for possession" because the tenant did any of the protected things — including that "[t]he tenant has complained to an appropriate governmental agency of a violation of a building, housing, health, or safety code that is applicable to the premises, and the violation materially affects health and safety," complained to the landlord about a 5321.04 violation, or joined with other tenants to negotiate collectively. Read that last clause carefully: under (A)(1) the code violation you reported has to be one that materially affects health and safety for the anti-retaliation protection to attach. A complaint to the landlord about a 5321.04 duty — which is what an entry problem is — travels under (A)(2) instead, and (A)(2) carries no such qualifier.

If it happens anyway, 5321.02(B) lets the tenant use the retaliatory action as a defense to an action for possession, recover possession or terminate the rental agreement, and recover actual damages together with reasonable attorney's fees. Frequently asked questions

Can a landlord enter my apartment without notice in Ohio?
Only in the narrow cases the statute names. ORC 5321.04(A)(8) begins "Except in the case of emergency or if it is impracticable to do so," then requires reasonable notice of intent to enter and entry only at reasonable times. Outside those two exceptions, notice is required, and 24 hours is presumed reasonable in the absence of evidence to the contrary.
Is a text message enough notice under Ohio law?
The statute says "reasonable notice" and does not name a delivery method — no writing requirement, no certified mail, nothing about text or email. A text that arrives 24 hours ahead and says what day, roughly what time, and why, will generally look reasonable. The practical advantage of text or email is that it timestamps itself, which matters if you ever have to prove what you were told and when.
Can my landlord show the house to new applicants while I still live there?
Yes. ORC 5321.05(B) says a tenant "shall not unreasonably withhold consent" for the landlord to enter to inspect, make repairs, deliver oversized parcels, supply agreed services, or exhibit the unit to prospective or actual purchasers, mortgagees, tenants, workmen or contractors. Showings are on that list. The entry still has to be noticed and at a reasonable time.
What can I do if my landlord keeps entering without notice?
ORC 5321.04(B) covers three situations: entry that violates (A)(8), a lawful entry made in an unreasonable manner, and repeated demands for entry that have the effect of harassing the tenant. For any of them the tenant may recover actual damages, obtain injunctive relief to prevent the conduct from recurring, and obtain a judgment for reasonable attorney's fees — or may terminate the rental agreement.
Can my landlord retaliate if I complain to the city?
ORC 5321.02(A) forbids it. A landlord may not raise rent, cut services due to the tenant, or bring or threaten an action for possession because the tenant complained to an appropriate governmental agency about a code violation that materially affects health and safety, complained to the landlord about a 5321.04 violation, or joined with other tenants to bargain collectively. The materially-affects-health-and-safety qualifier sits in (A)(1) only; a complaint made to the landlord under (A)(2) does not carry it. Under 5321.02(B) retaliation is a defense to an eviction and supports actual damages plus reasonable attorney's fees.
