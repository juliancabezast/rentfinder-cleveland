Can a Landlord Shut Off Utilities or Change the Locks in Ohio?

No. Ohio bans self-help eviction by name. ORC 5321.15(A): "No landlord of residential premises shall initiate any act, including termination of utilities or services, exclusion from the premises, or threat of any unlawful act, against a tenant … for the purpose of recovering possession of residential premises, other than as provided in Chapters 1923., 5303., and 5321." Section (B) separately forbids seizing your furnishings or possessions to collect rent without a court order, and (C) makes the landlord "liable in a civil action for all damages caused to a tenant … together with reasonable attorneys fees."

What ORC 5321.15 forbids: lockouts, utility interruption and seizing property

Most tenant-protection statutes are written in general terms and leave courts to fill in the conduct. ORC 5321.15 is different: the Ohio legislature listed the tactics.

  • (A) — No self-help to recover possession. "No landlord of residential premises shall initiate any act, including termination of utilities or services, exclusion from the premises, or threat of any unlawful act, against a tenant … for the purpose of recovering possession of residential premises, other than as provided in Chapters 1923., 5303., and 5321. of the Revised Code." Note the three named acts: cutting utilities, locking you out, and threatening something unlawful. A threat is enough — nothing has to actually be shut off.
  • (B) — No seizing your things for rent. "No landlord of residential premises shall seize the furnishings or possessions of a tenant … for the purpose of recovering rent payments, other than in accordance with an order issued by a court of competent jurisdiction."
  • (C) — The consequence. "A landlord who violates this section is liable in a civil action for all damages caused to a tenant … together with reasonable attorneys fees."

Read (A) and (B) together and you can see the design. (A) is about possession — anything done to get you out. (B) is about money — anything done to get paid. Between them they cover essentially every improvised remedy a frustrated landlord might reach for: pulling the meter, cancelling the water account, changing the deadbolt, removing an exterior door, putting your belongings on the tree lawn, or simply saying "be out by Friday or the power goes off."

None of it depends on whether you owe rent. Nothing in 5321.15 conditions the protection on being current. A tenant who has not paid in three months still cannot lawfully be locked out — the landlord's remedy is the courthouse, and only the courthouse.

The only lawful way to remove a tenant in Ohio: a Chapter 1923 eviction

The statute names its own alternative: Chapters 1923, 5303 and 5321. For a residential tenancy the relevant one is Chapter 1923, forcible entry and detainer. It has a fixed shape, and every step of it is visible from the outside — which is precisely why a lockout is such a clear violation. If none of this happened, no lawful eviction happened.

  • A written three-day notice. ORC 1923.04(A) requires notice to leave the premises "three or more days before beginning the action," and requires that the notice carry, conspicuously printed, this language: "You are being asked to leave the premises. If you do not leave, an eviction action may be initiated against you. If you are in doubt regarding your legal rights and obligations as a tenant, it is recommended that you seek legal assistance." In Cleveland, the Housing Court adds that the three days are business days, and that the day of service, weekends and holidays, and the filing date do not count.
  • A case filed in court, and a summons served. ORC 1923.06: "Such service shall be at least seven days before the day set for trial."
  • A hearing, and a judgment. You get a court date and the right to appear.
  • A writ of restitution, executed by an officer. ORC 1923.14: "Within ten days after receiving a writ of execution … the sheriff, police officer, constable, or bailiff shall execute it by restoring the plaintiff to the possession of the premises."

That last line is the tell. A lawful set-out in Ohio is performed by a public officer under a writ — not by the owner, not by a maintenance crew, not by a locksmith at 8 p.m. Our full walkthrough of the sequence and its timing is in the Ohio eviction process, step by step.

What to do in the first hour of a lockout or shutoff

Do not force the door and do not escalate. Do this instead, in this order:

  • Confirm and record the moment. Photograph the door, the new lock or the missing meter, and the time. If the power or gas is off, photograph the panel or the meter too.
  • Call the utility, in your name or not. Ask one question and write down the answer: who requested this and on what date? A shutoff ordered by the account holder is a record that exists, and it is the single most damaging document in a self-help case.
  • Text the landlord, once, in writing. Short and factual: "I am locked out of [address] as of [time/date]. ORC 5321.15 prohibits exclusion from the premises to recover possession. Please restore access today." You are creating an exhibit and giving a chance to cure at the same time.
  • Call the police for a report if you cannot get in. Officers generally will not settle a possession dispute on the sidewalk, but a report timestamps the lockout with a neutral third party.
  • Call the Cleveland Housing Court the same day — (216) 664-4295. This is the court with jurisdiction over the relationship, and getting into its process early is the point.
  • Keep paying rent if you can. Being current removes the landlord's only counter-narrative and preserves the repair remedies in ORC 5321.07, which require it.

Evidence that wins these cases: photos, texts, utility records, witness names

Self-help cases are usually won on paper, not on testimony, because the acts leave records in third-party systems that neither side controls.

  • The utility's own record of who ordered the disconnection and when. Request it in writing.
  • Timestamped photographs — the changed lock, the drilled cylinder, the removed door, the dark panel, your belongings outside.
  • The text thread. Do not delete anything, including messages that make you look impatient. Complete threads are credible; curated ones are not.
  • Proof of what it cost you. Hotel receipts, restaurant receipts because you had no kitchen, spoiled groceries, a locksmith, missed shifts, storage. ORC 5321.15(C) awards "all damages caused to a tenant" — and damages are proved with receipts.
  • Names of witnesses. The neighbor in the upstairs suite who watched the locksmith arrive is worth more than your own account of it.
  • A written chronology, kept as it happens, not reconstructed later.

Damages you can recover, including reasonable attorney fees

ORC 5321.15(C) is short and unusually generous: liability "in a civil action for all damages caused to a tenant … together with reasonable attorneys fees."

The fee provision is the part to understand. Ohio does not generally let a lease shift attorney's fees — ORC 5321.13(C) says "[n]o agreement to pay the landlord's or tenant's attorney's fees shall be recognized in any rental agreement for residential premises," and 5321.13(D) refuses to recognize any tenant agreement exculpating or limiting the landlord's liability or indemnifying the landlord for it. So the fees here do not come from a contract; they come from the statute, and a lease clause cannot take them away. That is what makes it realistic to find counsel for a case where the out-of-pocket loss is a few hundred dollars.

Layered on top, ORC 5321.12 provides that in any action under Chapter 5321, "any party may recover damages for the breach of contract or the breach of any duty that is imposed by law." And if the lockout followed a repair dispute, the entry provisions may also be in play: ORC 5321.04(B) covers entries in violation of the notice rule, lawful entries made in an unreasonable manner, and repeated harassing demands for entry.

Where to file: Cleveland Municipal Court, Housing Division

In the City of Cleveland, landlord-tenant matters go to the Housing Division of the Cleveland Municipal Court — the Cleveland Housing Court. Its civil filings are handled on the second floor of the Justice Center, 1200 Ontario Street, with the Civil Division of the Municipal Clerk of Courts, and the Housing Court's number is (216) 664-4295.

Two things worth knowing before you go. The Court publishes filing fees for evictions — $110.00 for a forcible entry and detainer with one defendant by certified mail, plus $7.00 per additional defendant, and $10.00 with a poverty affidavit — and its cost schedule also lists filings a tenant can bring, among them a Landlord-Tenant Complaint at $120.00 with bailiff service, a Money Only Complaint in the Housing Division at $110.00, and a Small Claims Complaint at $37.00, each with a reduced fee on a poverty affidavit. What that schedule does not list is a fee or a timeline for a rent deposit (escrow) filing specifically, so call and ask rather than assuming. The Court also staffs Housing Court Specialists, whom its own FAQ points tenants to for procedural information; they are the right first call.

If you live outside the City of Cleveland — East Cleveland, Lakewood, Euclid, Parma, or in Lorain or Summit County — your case belongs to that municipality's or county's court, not to Cleveland's. The statute is statewide; the courthouse is not.

When the utility is in your name and gets shut off for nonpayment

This is a different problem with a different fix, and mixing them up wastes time. If the account is yours and the utility disconnected you for nonpayment, no landlord violated 5321.15 — the door that opens is the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio's.

  • The Special Reconnect Order. PUCO issues it "on an annual basis typically mid October through mid April." "Any residential customer who is served by a regulated electric and or natural gas utility company, regardless of income, may pay $175 to use the Special Reconnect Order to establish, maintain, or restore service." Once per winter heating season, primary residence only; all regulated gas and electric companies must comply.
  • HEAP Winter Crisis. Available "November 1 through March 31" for households at or below 175 percent of the federal poverty guidelines who are facing disconnection, have been disconnected, need to establish or transfer service, are in PIPP default, need to pay a first PIPP, or have less than a 25 percent supply of bulk fuel.
  • A 30-day medical certificate. Residential customers of PUCO-regulated electric, natural gas and water utilities facing disconnection for nonpayment may use it to avoid disconnection for up to 30 days where the loss of service would pose a serious risk to a permanent resident's health or disable necessary medical or life-support equipment.
  • PIPP Plus, the ongoing plan: pay the monthly PIPP Plus amount on time and in full, and "a portion of their past-due balance, and the remaining amount of that month's bill, is forgiven as a credit."

The PUCO Call Center is (800) 686-7826. Preventing the whole scenario starts at move-in — see setting up utilities as a Cleveland renter.

Emergency help the same night

If you are locked out with nowhere to go, deal with tonight first and the claim second. Our same-day emergency housing page for Cleveland collects the intake routes. Keep every receipt from the night — they are damages.

For habitability problems that come with the lockout (no heat, no water, unsafe conditions), Cleveland's Building & Housing complaints run through 3-1-1 or (216) 664-2000, and the City instructs residents to call 911 to report the smell of gas.

If your belongings are still inside, say so, in writing, to the landlord and to the court. ORC 5321.15(B) is the provision that governs them, and the sooner it is on the record that property is being held, the better.

Retaliation: what protects you after you assert these rights

Renters hesitate because asserting a right feels like inviting an eviction. ORC 5321.02(A) anticipates that: a landlord may not retaliate "by increasing the tenant's rent, decreasing services that are due to the tenant, or bringing or threatening to bring an action for possession" because the tenant complained to an appropriate governmental agency about a building, housing, health or safety code violation, complained to the landlord about a 5321.04 violation, or "joined with other tenants for the purpose of negotiating or dealing collectively with the landlord on any of the terms and conditions of a rental agreement."

And division (B) of that same section sets out what a tenant can do when a landlord does retaliate: raise the retaliation as a defense if the landlord sues to recover possession, sue to recover possession of the premises, or terminate the rental agreement — and, on top of whichever route is taken, recover actual damages from the landlord together with reasonable attorney's fees (official text of ORC 5321.02).

Two more provisions worth knowing while you sort this out. Your security deposit is governed by ORC 5321.16(B) — itemized deductions and any amount due delivered within thirty days after termination and delivery of possession — with 5321.16(C) allowing recovery of the money plus damages equal to the amount wrongfully withheld plus fees; see our deposit guide. And if you decide to leave rather than fight, breaking a lease in Ohio covers how to do it without creating a new problem.

Frequently asked questions

Can my landlord turn off my water or electricity in Ohio?
No. ORC 5321.15(A) says no landlord of residential premises shall initiate any act, "including termination of utilities or services, exclusion from the premises, or threat of any unlawful act," against a tenant for the purpose of recovering possession, other than as provided in Chapters 1923, 5303 and 5321. Terminating utilities is named in the statute itself.
Is it legal for a landlord to change the locks in Ohio?
Not as a way to get you out. A lockout is "exclusion from the premises," which ORC 5321.15(A) prohibits when done to recover possession outside the court process. The only lawful route is an action under Chapter 1923, ending in a writ of restitution executed by a sheriff, police officer, constable or bailiff.
What damages can I recover for an illegal lockout in Ohio?
ORC 5321.15(C) states that a landlord who violates the section "is liable in a civil action for all damages caused to a tenant…together with reasonable attorneys fees." The attorney-fee provision is what makes these cases economically viable. ORC 5321.12 separately allows any party to recover damages for breach of contract or breach of any duty imposed by law.
Can a landlord remove my belongings while I am out?
ORC 5321.15(B) says no landlord shall seize the furnishings or possessions of a tenant for the purpose of recovering rent payments, other than in accordance with an order issued by a court of competent jurisdiction. Being behind on rent does not create a landlord's lien on your property in Ohio.
Who do I call for an illegal lockout in Cleveland?
The Cleveland Housing Court — the Housing Division of the Cleveland Municipal Court — is at (216) 664-4295, and its civil filings are handled on the second floor of the Justice Center at 1200 Ontario Street. If there is a code or habitability issue alongside the lockout, Cleveland's Building & Housing complaints go through 3-1-1 or (216) 664-2000. If you smell gas, call 911.
