What we rent in Milwaukee: single-family houses and duplex units

Our Milwaukee inventory is houses, not apartment complexes. That means detached single-family homes and the upper or lower flat of a two-family house — the duplex form that dominates Milwaukee's older residential blocks. When you rent a flat here you are renting a full unit with its own entrance, its own kitchen and bath, and usually a share of a basement and a yard, which is why we list them alongside detached houses rather than under "apartments."

The practical difference for you is in the utilities and the shared systems. In a two-family house the basement, the laundry hookups, the driveway and sometimes a single furnace or a single water meter are shared with the other flat. Federal voucher rules require the lease to spell this out: under 24 CFR §982.308(d)(5), an assisted lease must specify "what utilities and appliances are to be supplied by the owner, and what utilities and appliances are to be supplied by the family." Read that clause on any Milwaukee flat before you sign, voucher or not — it is the clause that decides whether you or the owner pays for heat in January.

Wisconsin backs that up with a hard disclosure rule. Before a landlord accepts a security deposit or signs you up, they must disclose known, uncorrected building or housing code violations that threaten health or safety — and the state's rental practices code specifically names heating that cannot hold "at least 67°F (19°C)" in all living areas during every season the unit can be occupied as one of the conditions that must be disclosed. In a Milwaukee winter that is not a technicality.

Browse by bedrooms: 2, 3 and 4 bedroom houses

Two- and three-bedroom houses are the core of what turns over in Milwaukee. Four-bedrooms exist but move fast, and if you are on a voucher there is a rule about them that costs people money every year.

Your voucher carries a family unit size — the bedroom count HACM assigns based on who is in your household, under the subsidy standards required by 24 CFR §982.402. A few specifics from that rule that people get wrong: a household of one person gets a zero- or one-bedroom voucher unless a live-in aide is approved; a pregnant applicant with no other household members "must be treated as a two-person family"; an approved live-in aide is counted; and a child temporarily in foster care still counts toward your family unit size.

Here is the part that matters when you are shopping. Under 24 CFR §982.505(c)(1), the payment standard applied to your family is the lower of the standard for your family unit size or the standard for the size of the unit you actually rent. Renting a four-bedroom house on a two-bedroom voucher does not get you a four-bedroom subsidy — you stay at the two-bedroom standard and pay the difference yourself. Renting a smaller unit than your voucher allows drops you to the smaller standard. The subsidy follows the smaller of the two numbers, always.

HACM's 2026 payment standard tabulator works by exception. One base schedule applies to "all zip codes except those listed below," and the listed exceptions are 53129, 53130, 53132, 53154, 53202, 53203, 53207, 53211, 53213, 53217, 53223, 53224, 53226, 53227 and 53228. That is a genuinely useful fact when you are comparing houses across the north and west sides: your voucher ceiling does not change from one of those ZIPs to the next. What changes is the rent the owner is asking and whether HACM will approve it, which is a separate test called rent reasonableness — more on that below.

On neighborhood names: renters and listing sites use "Sherman Park" loosely for a stretch of the west side that overlaps 53210 and neighboring ZIPs. Neighborhood boundaries and USPS ZIP boundaries are drawn by different bodies for different purposes and they do not line up, and the City of Milwaukee's own site would not serve us a boundary map we could cite. So we don't publish a street-by-street definition. Check the ZIP printed on the listing itself, because that — not the neighborhood label — is what HACM uses.

Housing Choice Vouchers: accepted on every house, and how HACM fits in

Every Milwaukee house we manage takes a voucher. That is our policy, and in Wisconsin it also lines up with the statute: Wis. Stat. §106.50(1m)(nm) lists "lawful source of income" among the protected classes alongside sex, race, color, disability, sexual orientation, religion, national origin, marital status, family status, age and ancestry. The agency that enforces it, the Department of Workforce Development's Equal Rights Division, defines source of income as "the lawful source of a person's income, including wages, a voucher having monetary value, social security, public assistance or other related payments." The statute's prohibited conduct list at §106.50(2)(a) includes "refusing to sell, rent, finance or contract to construct housing or by refusing to negotiate or discuss the terms thereof."

One caveat, stated honestly: the published annotations to §106.50 cite a 1995 federal decision, Knapp v. Eagle Property Management Corp., 54 F.3d 1272, for the proposition that federal rent vouchers "are not clearly within the meaning of" lawful source of income. That is a live legal tension between a court note and the enforcing agency's current guidance, and it is a question for a lawyer, not for a rental website. If you believe you were turned away in Milwaukee because of a voucher, the DWD route is a complaint filed within one year of the discriminatory action; if no probable cause is found you have 20 days to appeal in writing.

As for getting a voucher in the first place: as of August 8, 2026, HACM states plainly that "the Section 8 tenant-based rent assistance waiting list is currently CLOSED." Two doors are still open. HACM is accepting portability — if you hold a voucher from another housing authority, you can move it to Milwaukee, and you start that process with your current authority, not with HACM. And HACM's Public Housing and Project-Based Voucher waiting lists are open, with a deadline that matters right now: anyone already on those lists must update their information and confirm continued interest by August 16, 2026 through the RentCafé applicant portal at myportal.hacm.org, or lose their place.

What you need to apply in Wisconsin: documents, income proof, deposit

Bring photo ID, proof of income, and your voucher packet if you have one. Beyond that, Wisconsin has a handful of applicant protections most renters never hear about:

  • The credit report fee is capped. Under Wis. Stat. §704.085, a landlord may require "the landlord's actual cost, up to $25, to obtain a consumer credit report" — must notify you of the charge before requesting it, and must give you a copy of the report. If you hand over your own consumer report that is less than 30 days old, they cannot charge you at all.
  • Prepaid rent can legally be a deposit. Wisconsin's rental practices code defines "security deposit" to include "all rent payments in excess of 1 month's prepaid rent." So if someone asks for first, last and security, the "last" is a deposit and is subject to the 21-day return rule.
  • You get an inspection window before the deposit is locked in. Before accepting a security deposit, the landlord must notify you in writing that you may inspect the unit and report preexisting damage, with a deadline "not less than 7 days after the start of tenancy."
  • Every residential lease must carry the domestic abuse notice. Wis. Stat. §704.14 requires the NOTICE OF DOMESTIC ABUSE PROTECTIONS in the lease or an addendum. Its absence is a sign the landlord is not using a current Wisconsin form.

Wisconsin renter basics: the check-in sheet, the 21-day deposit rule, who fixes what

Wisconsin's landlord-tenant rules are stricter and more specific than most renters expect, and they are enforced through a consumer-protection statute with real teeth. The short version:

  • The check-in sheet is mandatory. Wis. Stat. §704.08: the landlord must give a new residential tenant a check-in sheet when occupancy starts, and you get 7 days to complete and return it. Fill it in. Photograph everything. It is the single cheapest thing you will ever do to protect a deposit.
  • 21 days for the deposit. The landlord must deliver or mail the full deposit, less lawful deductions, within 21 days — counted from lease termination, or from the start of a new tenancy if the place is re-rented early, or from the date the landlord learns you left, depending on the situation.
  • No deducting for normal wear. The code states outright that it "does not authorize a landlord to withhold any amount from a security deposit for normal wear and tear." If anything is withheld, you must get a written statement, within the same 21 days, describing each item and the amount charged for it.
  • Double damages. A person who suffers a monetary loss from a violation of the rental practices code "may sue the violator directly under s. 100.20 (5), Stats., and may recover twice the amount of the loss, together with costs and reasonable attorneys' fees."
  • 12 hours' notice to enter. Advance notice for inspection, repair or showing means at least 12 hours unless you agree to less. The exceptions are narrow: your advance consent, a health or safety emergency, or your absence plus a reasonable belief entry is needed to protect the property from damage.
  • Lockouts are illegal. "No landlord may exclude, forcibly evict or constructively evict a tenant from a dwelling unit, other than by an eviction procedure specified under ch. 799, Stats."
  • Retaliation is illegal for reporting a code violation, organizing with other tenants, or exercising a legal right.
  • Repairs cannot be waived away. Wisconsin requires the landlord to keep the structure and the systems they agreed to supply in reasonable repair and to comply with the local housing code — and "an agreement to waive the requirements of this section in a residential tenancy, including an agreement in a rental agreement, is void."
  • But do not withhold full rent. If a condition materially affects health or safety, rent abates "to the extent the tenant is deprived of the full normal use of the premises." The statute adds: "This section does not authorize rent to be withheld in full, if the tenant remains in possession." Renters lose eviction cases over this every year.

Late fees are also constrained: a landlord may only charge one if the lease specifically provides for it, must first apply any rent prepayments you have made, and may never charge a fee for nonpayment of a late fee.

Frequently asked questions

Does Rent Finder accept Section 8 vouchers in Milwaukee?
Yes. Every house we manage in Milwaukee accepts Housing Choice Vouchers. Wisconsin also lists lawful source of income as a protected class under Wis. Stat. §106.50, and the state agency that enforces the housing law, the DWD Equal Rights Division, defines that class as including "a voucher having monetary value."
How much is rent for a house in Milwaukee?
Our Milwaukee houses generally rent in the $700 to $1,800 a month range depending on bedroom count and condition. For voucher holders the practical ceiling is HACM's 2026 payment standard, which for most Milwaukee ZIP codes is $1,230 for a one-bedroom, $1,471 for a two-bedroom and $1,812 for a three-bedroom.
How do I schedule a showing for a Milwaukee rental?
Book online through our showing page or call (440) 444-4737. Milwaukee showing times are shown in Central Time, which is one hour behind our Cleveland office, so confirm the time zone when you book by phone.
What documents do I need to rent a house in Milwaukee?
Photo ID, proof of income, and your voucher paperwork if you have one. How long does a Milwaukee rental application take?
Screening itself is usually a few business days. If you are using a voucher, the longer step is HACM: you submit a request for tenancy approval with a copy of the lease, and federal rules require the unit to be inspected before the initial lease term begins, so build extra time into your move date.
